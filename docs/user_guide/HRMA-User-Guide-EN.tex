\section{What is HRMA?}\label{sec:what}

HRMA (Rocket Motor Analysis Suite) is a desktop application for the
preliminary design and analysis of hybrid, solid, and liquid rocket motors.
It runs a calculation engine on your own machine, inside its own application
window, and presents the results in a dark interactive interface: a
performance summary, a warnings panel, dimensioned design tables, an
interactive 3D model built from the solver output, an Analysis Dock of
engineering panels, and working CAD, drawing, and report exports.

\subsection{What the program does}

You give HRMA an operating point (thrust, burn time, chamber pressure,
propellants) and the hardware choices that go with it (materials, wall
thickness, nozzle type, injector type). HRMA sizes the motor, predicts its
performance, draws it, and tells you where the design sits against the
limits of its own models.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em]
  \item \textbf{Three designer pages:} Hybrid, Solid, and Liquid, each with
  its own input form, results, and exports.
  \item \textbf{An Analysis Dock:} fourteen engineering panels (structural,
  thermal, flow, feed system, safety, uncertainty, validation, and more)
  that run on the motor you just calculated.
  \item \textbf{An Analysis Center:} a component-by-analysis matrix for the
  heavier solves (finite-element structural and thermal, plane-strain grain,
  nozzle CFD, chamber acoustics, combustion stability).
  \item \textbf{Exports:} STEP, STL, DXF, technical-drawing PDF, report PDF,
  Excel and CSV tables, and an OpenRocket-compatible \texttt{.eng} motor
  file.
  \item \textbf{A launch site page:} pick a point on the globe and fly the
  motor you calculated along a solved flight path.
  \item \textbf{A formula reference:} the relations the solvers work from,
  typeset and grouped into fourteen sections.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Who it is for}

Rocket teams, undergraduate and graduate students, faculty, and anyone who
wants to follow motor physics end to end. The program does not design a
motor \emph{for} you: it turns the parameters you enter into geometry,
performance numbers, and warnings, which you then judge with engineering
reasoning.

\subsection{Everything runs on your computer}

The calculation engine, the propellant and material databases, the chart
library, and the 3D viewer are all bundled with the application. No design
data is uploaded anywhere, and the program works with the network cable
unplugged. There are exactly two places where HRMA can reach the internet,
and you control both:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em]
  \item \textbf{The update check at startup}, which asks GitHub whether a
  newer version exists. If it fails, the program continues normally, and you
  can switch the check off in Settings.
  \item \textbf{The \emph{Use online enrichment (Open-Meteo)} checkbox} on
  the Launch Site page, which is off unless you tick it. With it off, the
  site is resolved from local data only.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{The rule the whole program follows: fail closed}

HRMA does not invent a number it cannot compute. This is the single habit
that explains most of what you will see on screen:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em]
  \item If an input is missing, the program does not substitute a plausible
  value silently. Either it refuses the request and names the field, or it
  applies a canonical default \emph{and declares it by name} in the
  ``assumed on your behalf'' block.
  \item If a model cannot cover your case, the result says so instead of
  producing a number. You will see \texttt{NOT MODELLED}, ``no verdict
  published'', \texttt{NOT EVALUATED}, or ``no published data'' where an
  answer would have been guesswork.
  \item If a verdict cannot be earned, it is withheld even when the
  underlying number is still shown. A drawing is not produced from a
  dimension the program had to assume.
  \item Every published value carries its source: \texttt{COMPUTED},
  \texttt{USER}, \texttt{ASSUMED}, or \texttt{NOT MODELLED}.
\end{itemize}

\begin{notebox}
\small\color{hrmadark}
\textbf{Read the gaps as information.} An empty field with a reason next to
it is the program telling you that your design is outside what it can
honestly cover. That is more useful than a number you cannot trace.
\end{notebox}

\subsection{How to use this guide}

Sections~\ref{sec:install} and~\ref{sec:pages} get you installed and
oriented. Section~\ref{sec:example} works through one complete example motor,
a hybrid burning HTPB with liquid N\textsubscript{2}O at 1000~N for 10~s, and
the steps generalize to the other two motor types.

Sections~\ref{sec:design} and~\ref{sec:reading} are the engineering core of
this guide. Section~\ref{sec:design} takes a mission statement and turns it
into a motor, decision by decision, naming at each step the field you type
into, the number the program publishes back, and the band that number has to
land in. Section~\ref{sec:reading} is the other half: how to read what comes
back, which of the three performance numbers blames which piece of hardware,
how far the models have been measured to be off, and how to tell an earned
verdict from a read-back.

Sections~\ref{sec:dock} to~\ref{sec:exports} cover the analysis panels, the
motor-type differences, and the exports. Sections~\ref{sec:inputrules}
and~\ref{sec:mistakes} are the troubleshooting pair: what the program refuses
and why, then the field guide to what you actually see on screen when it does.
Section~\ref{sec:scope} is the one to read before you trust a number: what the
models have actually been validated against.
Appendix~\ref{sec:appc} is the field-by-field reference, with the accepted
range for every input and what happens when you leave it empty.

All screenshots come from the application itself. Field names in this guide
are written exactly as they appear on screen in the English interface.

\section{Installation and First Launch}\label{sec:install}

\subsection{Installing}

Each release ships two installers. Both embed a Python runtime and every
library HRMA needs (thermochemistry, CAD kernel, fluid properties, charts),
so you do not install Python or download anything at first run.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em]
  \item \textbf{Windows 10/11:} download \texttt{HRMA-Setup-3.0.0.exe},
  double-click it, and follow the wizard. The installation is per-user (it
  goes under your own \texttt{LOCALAPPDATA}), so no administrator rights are
  required. Windows SmartScreen may warn you because the installer is not
  code-signed: choose ``More info'', then ``Run anyway''.
  \item \textbf{macOS 11+ (Apple Silicon):} open
  \texttt{HRMA-Setup-3.0.0-macOS.dmg} and drag \texttt{HRMA} into
  \texttt{Applications}. The macOS build is for Apple Silicon and does not
  run on Intel Macs.
\end{itemize}

\begin{warnbox}
\small\color{hrmadark}
\textbf{Unsigned packages.} Neither installer carries a paid developer
signature, so the operating system asks you to confirm the first launch
(SmartScreen on Windows, Gatekeeper on macOS). The macOS application bundle
itself is signed locally and the release process verifies that signature
before publishing; an unsigned bundle stops the release.
\end{warnbox}

\subsection{First launch}

HRMA opens its window immediately and shows a loading page while the
calculation engines load in the background; the home page replaces it as
soon as the engine answers. You get three motor-type cards, a version badge,
and the links in the top bar.

\fig{img/en-00-home.png}{The home page: cards for Hybrid Motors, Solid
Motors and Liquid Engines, the Formula Reference link, and the top-bar
controls (Language, Release Notes, User Guide, Settings).}

If HRMA is already running, starting it again does not open a second copy;
the existing window is used. The application picks a free local port for
itself, so nothing needs to be configured.

\subsection{The top bar and the window menu}

Four controls follow you from page to page:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}>{\raggedright}p{3.4cm} p{10.6cm}@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Control} & \textbf{What it does} \\
\midrule
\textbf{Language} & Switches the entire interface between \texttt{English}
and \texttt{Türkçe}, chart labels included. The Settings panel keeps the
same setting in sync. \\
\textbf{Release Notes} & Opens the release notes for the installed version
in a panel. \\
\textbf{User Guide} & Opens this guide as a PDF in your system PDF viewer,
in the language the interface is currently set to. \\
\textbf{Settings} & Opens the settings panel (language, updates, version
information, bug report). \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The same actions, plus \emph{Check for Updates\ldots} and \emph{Open Output
Folder}, are available from the native window menu, which is where to look
if the page has not finished loading. Appendix~A lists the menu items and
where each one sits on macOS and on Windows.

\subsection{Where your files go}

Everything HRMA writes for you goes into a single folder,
\texttt{Documents/HRMA}, with exported CAD files under
\texttt{Documents/HRMA/cad\_exports}. \emph{Open Output Folder} in the
window menu takes you straight there. Saved projects, exported drawings,
reports, and the application log all live under that folder, so backing up
one directory backs up your work.

\section{What Changed in v3.0}\label{sec:v30}

This section describes the changes a user coming from 2.6.26 will notice first
on screen in v3.0. (The work announced in the application as ``2.6.27'' ships
with this release; no separate 2.6.27 was published.) Every screenshot here was taken from the v3.0 application
itself, with the example projects; none has been edited. The concepts are
explained in more depth elsewhere: reading the results in
Section~\ref{sec:reading}, the Analysis Dock in Section~\ref{sec:dock}, exports
in Section~\ref{sec:exports}.

\subsection{Outside the model validity limit: ``verdict withheld''}

The structural model evaluates the wall at a given temperature. If that
temperature reaches the melting point of the material, or goes past the last
point of its strength-derating data, the strength is no longer known. In that
case HRMA does two things:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em]
  \item It shows the \textbf{OUTSIDE MODEL VALIDITY} badge and writes the reason
  codes:
  \begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em]
    \item \texttt{wall\_temperature\_at\_or\_above\_melting\_point}: the wall
    is at or above its melting point;
    \item \texttt{wall\_temperature\_above\_derating\_curve\_data}: the wall
    is above its strength-derating data.
  \end{itemize}
  \item It \textbf{withholds} the acceptance and the numeric design verdict. The
  safety-factor row shows \textbf{VERDICT WITHHELD} instead of a number; no
  PASS, SAFE or ``verified'' acceptance is shown.
\end{itemize}

Stresses, safety factors, wall thickness and masses stay on screen, but they are
\emph{diagnostic} only: they are not valid design outputs. A FAIL, UNSAFE or
MARGINAL status from the current model is never suppressed; it stays visible as
a warning.

\fig{img/v3_0/en-v30-01-kati-withheld.png}{Solid motor, Structural Analysis
tab: aluminium case, no insulation, long end-burner grain. The case heat model
finds the case above its melting point; the safety-factor row reads ``VERDICT
WITHHELD'' and two reason codes are listed.}

The same rule applies on every surface that carries a verdict: the result
cards, the end closures, the acceptance sentences of the sizing mode and the
structural panel of the Dock. The Dock panel reads the motor's own validity flag
and states where the flag came from, for example
\texttt{model\_validity\_limit\_exceeded true · motor.structural\_analysis.case\_analysis}.

\fig{img/v3_0/en-v30-02-dock-yapisal.png}{Analysis Dock, STRUCTURAL tab, same
motor: the ``OUTSIDE MODEL VALIDITY'' and ``VERDICT WITHHELD'' badges, the
source of the flag and the ``Basis of this verdict'' block. The STRUCTURE:
UNSAFE status stays visible.}

\begin{notebox}
\small\color{hrmadark}
\textbf{What this means, and what it does not.} When the wall temperature comes
from the heat-transfer module it is a \emph{bare-wall} steady-state estimate in
which protection by the grain, liner or insulation is not modelled. An
outside-validity result is a limit of the current model; it is not a statement
that a real motor of this design is impossible. To bring the result inside the
validity limit, revisit the material, the insulation, the cooling or the wall
temperature. For motors inside the validity limit, verdicts and acceptance
sentences appear as usual.
\end{notebox}

The safety panel follows the same principle. For propellant classes without a
sourced TNT equivalent (hybrid, sugar propellant, black powder) the TNT
equivalent, blast distance, fragment range and evacuation distance are shown as
\textbf{NOT\_EVALUATED}, with the reason named. No number is invented for these
classes any more.

\subsection{Thermal results: allowable temperature from the material record}

The thermal panel of the Dock plots the wall temperatures together with two
limits of the material: the \emph{allowable temperature} and the \emph{melting
point}. Both come from the record of the selected material. The note limit on
the heat-flux chart also comes from the allowable step. In the screenshot below
the limits for Aluminium 6061 are 477~K allowable and 855~K melting; the wall is
far above both, so the thermal risk is HIGH and the ``WALL T UNPHYSICAL ---
cooling insufficient'' badge is red.

\fig{img/v3_0/en-v30-03-dock-termal.png}{Analysis Dock, THERMAL tab, same solid
motor: risk and cooling badges, wall-temperature cards and the ``Wall
Temperatures vs Material Limits'' chart.}

The ``Heat Load \& Cooling Assessment'' table under the panel states the basis
next to each number. For example, when a value rests on a generic default exit
diameter this is shown next to the row with a
\texttt{generic\_defaults\_used} note.

\subsection{Panel provenance notes}

In v3.0 the panels of the Dock and the Analysis Center write \emph{what a
result rests on} next to the result. These lines are provenance notes. They
tell you which field a value was read from (its source path), whether a default
was applied, which inputs the result is conditional on, and what was not
measured.

\fig{img/v3_0/en-v30-05-panel-kunye.png}{UNCERTAINTY panel, example solid
motor: the ``What this P5--P95 band is conditional on'' table. The band comes
only from the distributions of the listed inputs; model-form error is not
sampled, and the band is not a confidence interval on true motor
performance.}

Other examples:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em]
  \item \textbf{Stability panel (chug):} the sensitivity to the choice of the
  time lag $\tau$ is shown under its own heading. When the result depends on the
  choice of $\tau$, the frequency and growth-rate bands across the $\tau$
  candidates are given; when it was not measured the panel says ``NOT
  MEASURED'', which is not the same as ``insensitive''. A source line names the
  block the value was read from.
  \item \textbf{Transient panel (hybrid blowdown):} when the burn is cut off, the
  oxidizer left in the tank and the impulse lost at cut-off appear as badges
  together with their field names. If a value is not published, the badge is
  not shown at all; it is never printed as ``0''.
  \item Panel texts, badges, table headings and chart axes follow the selected
  interface language. Machine field names (e.g.
  \texttt{model\_validity\_reasons}) stay the same in both languages so that
  they can be searched for in a bug report.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Hybrid warnings}

The warnings list on the hybrid page grew in v3.0. For the example hybrid
project (N$_2$O/HTPB) you see the following after Calculate:

\fig{img/v3_0/en-v30-04-hibrit-uyarilar.png}{Warning list of the example hybrid
motor: injector correlation range and pressure drop, the measured bias of the
HTPB regression coefficients, throat and wall temperature (CRITICAL), fields the
model solves itself, cavitation and flash-boiling risk.}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em]
  \item \textbf{N$_2$O inlet state:} the tank temperature and phase of the
  oxidizer are now inputs; the default is saturated liquid at 293.15~K. The
  vapour pressure $P_v$ in the cavitation warning is computed from this
  temperature.
  \item \textbf{Tank pressure in self-pressurised N$_2$O mode:} the injector
  circuit takes its feed pressure from the saturation pressure at the tank
  temperature, $P_{sat}(T_{tank})$. If the tank pressure you enter differs from
  it by more than the HRMA tolerance, a ``Tank pressure \dots{} was not used''
  warning appears; to change the feed pressure, change the oxidizer
  temperature. This is why the example project carries its tank pressure at
  $P_{sat}$.
  \item \textbf{Injector:} a Reynolds number outside the validity range of the
  correlation and a pressure drop that is too large relative to chamber
  pressure are reported separately.
  \item \textbf{Regression coefficients:} the measured bias of the built-in HTPB
  coefficients against the validation database is written out campaign by
  campaign. The coefficients are deliberately not re-tuned; you can enter your
  own $a$ and $n$ from your own firing data.
  \item \textbf{Fields the model solves:} for a field that is not read from the
  form but solved by the model (e.g. the expansion ratio) the warning states the
  value the analysis used.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{CAD export gate and refusal messages}

If a pressure-bearing part (chamber, nozzle, tanks) is not cleared by the
analysis, HRMA \textbf{does not write its manufacturing file}. The
manufacturing STEP package, the DXF manufacturing drawing of the same package,
the complete design package and the tank CAD package are refused on the
server; no file reaches the browser. The gate closes when:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em]
  \item the safety factor is below 1.0;
  \item the safety factor is not published or is not a finite number;
  \item the structural result is outside the model validity limit;
  \item the safety factor belongs to a wall thickness other than the drawn one.
\end{itemize}

\fig{img/v3_0/en-v30-06-cad-ret.png}{Example hybrid motor, ``Generate All
Files'': the manufacturing safety gate refuses the package for 2 parts. Each
reason is written with the part name, the number and the field the number was
read from.}

The refusal message is not a raw server response. It starts with the machine
code, then a summary sentence, then the reasons part by part. The machine code
for this gate is:
\begin{center}\texttt{manufacturing\_safety\_gate\_closed}\end{center} The message does not echo back the text you
sent.

There is a separate gate for the grain section. The CAD generators can draw
only a single circular port. A star or finocyl grain, or a multi-port grain, is
therefore not drawn as a plain tube; the export is refused with a ``fuel grain
section cannot be drawn'' reason. The tank CAD follows the flat-ended tank model
used by the solver. The nozzle STEP is a smooth surface of revolution through
the contour points; no conical facets show in the CAD program.

\subsection{Launch Site map: detail, Turkish names, privacy switch}

The globe on the Launch Site page gained offline vector detail in v3.0:
coastlines, country and province borders, lakes and rivers, city and country
labels. As you zoom in, a more detailed level (Natural Earth data at 1:110,
1:50 and 1:10 million) is loaded. This data ships with the application; no
internet is needed.

\fig{img/v3_0/en-v30-07-harita.png}{Launch Site, around Cyprus (camera altitude
458~km): the packaged offline Earth imagery, place names (``Dhekelia
Sovereign Base Area'', ``Akrotiri Sovereign Base Area'', ``Famagusta''),
provenance notes at
the bottom right, the ``0 MB · 0 tile'' cache counter and the ``Online
satellite tiles'' switch, off.}

\textbf{Turkish official names.} Place and country names are corrected to
Turkish official usage. In the Turkish interface this gives, for example,
Güney Kıbrıs Rum Yönetimi, Dikelya, Elazığ, Akmescit, Gözleve, Hankendi, İdlib,
Bükreş and Temeşvar; in the English interface the country label reads
``Türkiye''. The disputed boundary line in Crimea has been removed; Crimean
settlements are shown as part of Ukraine. The provenance note at the bottom
right states how many names were corrected and how many lines were removed.

\textbf{City dots.} A city dot marks an administrative or population point, not
the city limits. Lines are generalised data; at close range their positions can
be off by hundreds of metres up to kilometres.

\textbf{Privacy switch.} Satellite tiles come from NASA GIBS. Every tile request
tells the provider which area you are looking at. The ``Online satellite tiles
(NASA GIBS)'' switch is therefore \emph{off by default}:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em]
  \item \textbf{When off,} no request leaves the computer. The packaged NASA
  Blue Marble texture that ships with the application (December 2004, about
  2~km per pixel at the equator) is drawn without any network; areas not in
  the map cache show this texture or the global base texture. In the session
  of the screenshot the packaged tiles came from the local server (HTTP 200),
  the other tile requests were refused locally (HTTP 403) and no request
  reached the outside network.
  \item \textbf{When you switch it on,} a warning text appears and tiles are
  requested below about 1,500~km altitude. The state of the switch is stored in
  this browser; if it cannot be read, it is treated as off.
  \item The ``Clear map cache'' button deletes downloaded tiles; the counter next
  to it shows the cache size.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Other changes you will notice on screen}

The changes below went in just before v3.0 was released. The full list is in
the in-app Release Notes window and in \texttt{CHANGELOG.md}.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em]
  \item \textbf{The regenerative cooling panel no longer invents values.} The
  panel used to pre-fill values that had no source: channel count, channel
  width and depth, liner thickness, coolant, inlet temperature and inlet
  pressure. Now every field either takes the value the engine publishes or
  stays \textbf{empty and required}. No request is sent while a required field
  is empty. In practice this means: if the engine does not publish the channel
  dimensions, \emph{you have to enter} the channel count, channel width and
  depth. On the API, a \texttt{/api/regen-cooling} request without
  \texttt{n\_channels} gets a named 400 error. When the coolant station march
  cannot run, the thermal verdict does not silently disappear; a warning says
  so.
  \item \textbf{No English left-overs and no point decimals in Turkish mode.}
  Fixed places: the Structural tab of the solid motor (risk classes, closure
  bolt note), the structural and thermal panels of the Analysis Dock, the
  vehicle provenance and upload warnings on the Launch Site, the conditions of
  the uncertainty panel and the material names in hybrid warnings. Percentages
  are written in Turkish form (for example \%16,2).
  \item \textbf{The safety panel says why the thermal axis was not
  evaluated.} In an uncooled hybrid the heat module marks its own equilibrium
  wall temperature as non-physical. The panel does not give a thermal verdict
  from that temperature; the thermal verdict and the combined
  pressure-plus-thermal safety factor are withheld with the reason. The panel
  now states that reason; before, the case looked like a missing input.
  \item \textbf{The explosive equivalent follows the panel's rule in the API
  and the PDF report too.} For classes without a sourced TNT equivalent
  (hybrid, sugar propellants such as KNDX, black powder) the TNT equivalent
  and the distances derived from it become ``not evaluated'' in the API
  response and in the PDF report as well. The solid propellant class is taken
  from the catalogue family, so KNDX is no longer counted as a composite
  propellant. APCP and liquid results are unchanged.
  \item \textbf{The uncertainty analysis runs with a zero diameter input.} A
  zero diameter means ``automatic'', as in the engine calculation. The
  uncertainty run of the example hybrid engine used to fail with
  \texttt{non\_physical\_uncertainty\_input}; it now runs.
  \item \textbf{Broken export data gets a named refusal.} When an inner block
  of the export payload (for example \texttt{design\_summary} or
  \texttt{structural\_analysis}) is not in the expected form, the export
  endpoints no longer return a server error (HTTP 500). Instead they return a
  422 \texttt{invalid\_export\_geometry} that names the full path of the field.
  The STL package returns the assembly builder's named refusal (422) when the
  throat or exit diameter is not published. The manufacturing gate described
  above covers, besides STEP and DXF, the drawing PDF, the STL package, the
  single STL, the complete package and the \texttt{/api/export-cad} endpoint.
  \item \textbf{The CFD runtime note follows the selected mode.} A viscous run
  now quotes the worst-case runtime table for viscous runs; before, it quoted
  the value from the inviscid table. Both tables were re-measured with a single
  protocol. The default resolution level measured 18.5~s without numba. The
  viscous table was measured on a loaded machine and is therefore a cautious
  upper bound; the note says so.
\end{itemize}

\fig{img/en-01-form.png}{The top of the hybrid input form: Motor
Information, Flight Environment and Basic Parameters. The form continues
below with the propellant, injector and design sections, where \emph{Find

\textbf{Empty, zero, and automatic are three different things.} This
distinction matters more on this page than any other:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em]
  \item \textbf{Empty} means ``I am not sending this''. An empty field is
  not transmitted at all, so the server can tell the difference between a
  value you left out and a value you deliberately set. Where the endpoint
  has a canonical default it applies it and lists it by name in the
  declaration block; where it has none, it refuses and tells you which field
  it needs.
  \item \textbf{Zero} is an explicit instruction on the fields that document
  it, such as \emph{Initial Port Diameter (mm) 0 = auto}, \emph{Ae/At Ratio
  expansion ratio, 0=auto} and \emph{Chamber Diameter (mm) 0=auto}. There,
  \texttt{0} tells the solver to size the quantity itself.
  \item \textbf{A number} is used as given, after the band check described
  in Section~\ref{sec:inputrules}.
\end{itemize}

The \emph{Advanced Parameters} section holds the sizing levers: L* Value (m),
Initial Port Diameter, Number of Ports, Ae/At Ratio, Nozzle Type, Combustion
Type (infinite or finite area), Chamber Diameter, Contraction Ratio Ac/At,
and Mass Flux. The \emph{Fuel Configuration} section lets you override the
database values for fuel density and the regression-rate coefficients
$a$ and $n$; do that whenever you have coefficients fitted from your own
static fires.

\subsection{Step 2 --- Fill in the design configuration}

The form above sets the operating point. \emph{Configure Design} opens the
\emph{Design Configuration} panel, which says what the motor is made of and
what margin it is sized to. Two tabs: \emph{Motor Design} and \emph{Injector
Design}.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em]
  \item \textbf{Chamber Material} (AISI 304 Stainless Steel, Aluminum
  6061-T6, Inconel 718), \textbf{Wall Thickness (mm)} and the cooling
  selection feed the heat-transfer model (wall temperatures, heat flux) and
  the structural module, so the thermal and structural results describe the
  same motor. The first entry, AISI 304 stainless steel, is what the
  analysis uses unless you change it. Selecting cooling channels raises a
  warning: the coolant-side film coefficient is taken from the regenerative
  range in the literature, and coolant flow rate, channel velocity, pressure
  drop and boiling margin are not verified here. That is a declared limit of
  the model, not a fault.
  \item \textbf{Safety Factor:} the design factor the structural module aims
  at. Your real wall thickness is passed with it, so the module runs in
  verification mode and the reported minimum safety factor follows from
  pressure, diameter, material and thickness. A value outside the accepted
  range is refused with the range named, and a value that cannot be read at
  all is refused as not a number, in both cases without silently
  substituting a design value.
  \item \textbf{Chamber Length Override (mm):} overrides the chamber length
  derived from L*. Leave it empty for automatic. If your value is shorter
  than the fuel grain plus the pre-combustion chamber, the override is
  rejected and the warning quotes both your value and the minimum. It is not
  quietly trimmed, because a trimmed length would be a different motor from
  the one you asked for. A value above the accepted limit is rejected too,
  with the note that numbers that large usually mean a unit mix-up.
  \item \textbf{Nozzle Material} (graphite, tungsten, copper): drives the
  throat thermal check and the erosion estimate. The equilibrium wall
  temperature at the throat station is compared with the material's
  allowable temperature, and exceeding it is a critical warning that names
  both temperatures. Erosion is computed only for materials with a published
  coefficient band; tungsten has none, so no coefficient is invented and the
  result reads ``no published data''. The copper option is labelled
  regeneratively cooled and is solved under that assumption. Erosion is
  reported, not fed back: the steady-state solution assumes a rigid throat,
  so use the transient solver for erosion-coupled thrust and pressure
  histories.
  \item \textbf{Injector Material} (AISI 316 Stainless Steel, Titanium Grade
  5, Brass (Low Pressure)): sizes the injector plate. With the material's yield strength and density, the plate
  bending stress, safety factor, required thickness and mass are reported
  (edge-fixed circular plate, Roark's Formulas Table 11.2 case 10b; ASME
  BPVC PG-52 ligament efficiency for the drilled area).
  \item \textbf{Swirl injector fields:} if you select the \emph{Swirl}
  injector, \emph{Swirl Chamber Diameter} and \emph{Tangential Entry Angle}
  enter the pressure-swirl solution: the chamber diameter is the $D_s$ in
  $K = A_p/(D_s\,d_o)$, and the entry angle is taken as the target spray
  half-angle, from which the inverse solver picks $K$ and sizes the exit
  orifice and the slots. Leave the slot width and height \emph{empty} to
  design for a target angle. Fixing the slot geometry fixes the swirl number
  as well, so the target angle can no longer be honored, and the program
  says so rather than swallowing the input. Angles outside the solvable
  envelope are clamped with a warning, and a swirl chamber narrower than
  1.25 times the exit orifice breaks the geometric assumption of the model,
  which is also reported.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Step 3 --- Calculate}

Press \textbf{Calculate}. The form is sent, the solver runs, and the results
appear below. Four blocks matter:

\textbf{Performance Summary.} The headline numbers: thrust, total impulse,
specific impulse, characteristic velocity $c^*$, thrust coefficient $C_F$,
chamber pressure, throat and exit diameters, mass flows, and a safety-state
badge. The badge text comes from the result, not from a fixed list, and
nothing is printed before a calculation exists.

\textbf{Warnings.} Design-criteria messages from the solver and the
validation layer. \textbf{Read this panel before you trust anything above
it.} The messages are specific: a characteristic velocity outside the
expected band for your propellant pair names the band; a low expansion ratio
names the expected range; a chamber material that is not in the database
names both what you asked for and what was used instead; flow separation in
the nozzle states that thrust is reported on the separated effective
expansion ratio rather than the geometric one. If the panel is empty, no
warning was raised; it is not hidden when there is something to say.

\fig{img/en-07-warnings.png}{The Warnings panel. Each message names the
quantity, the band it is compared against, and what the solver did.}

\textbf{Design tables.} \emph{Motor Design} lists the full geometry
(parameter, value, unit): nozzle contour dimensions and angles, grain and
port geometry, chamber length with a statement of whether it came from L* or
from your override, and wall thickness. \emph{Injector Design} lists the
orifice plan. \emph{Design Report} summarizes the run.

\textbf{Material verdicts.} The throat temperature margin and erosion
estimate for the nozzle material, and the plate stress, safety factor,
required thickness and mass for the injector material. If a quantity cannot
be produced, nothing is invented: the block reports that it was not analyzed
and gives the reason. In particular, when the detailed injector circuit
model cannot size the type you selected, the orifice plan (hole count, hole
diameter, manifold diameter) is \emph{not reported} rather than estimated,
and the warning tells you not to take injector geometry from that run.

\fig{img/en-02-results.png}{The Performance Summary, the Warnings panel and
the design tables after a calculation. The values shown are the ones this
example produced; your own run prints its own numbers.}

\begin{warnbox}
\small
\textbf{This example is deliberately not a ``safe'' design.} With the values
above the structural verdict comes out \textbf{UNSAFE} (minimum safety factor
below~1), and the expansion ratio stays below~4 at the sea-level design point,
which raises the geometry band warning. The injector orifice Reynolds number
also comes out at 259{,}421, above the 200{,}000 upper edge of the
correlation, which raises a second band warning. Five further messages carry
the red CRITICAL badge: the graphite throat reaches 3313~K against a 3300~K
allowable, and the chamber wall reaches 3323~K against the 1673~K melting
point of AISI~304. The example is not thermally buildable as it stands;
Section~\ref{sec:design} works the structural and nozzle levers first. These
are the places where the program stops you, and it does not hide them:
changing the wall thickness, the chamber pressure and the expansion ratio to
see how the verdict turns is the whole point of this guide.

The O/F ratio is worth a note of its own, because the band moved in this
version. The page default of 7.5 is the tabulated optimum for the
N\textsubscript{2}O/HTPB pair, and it used to fall outside the validation band,
so a user who opened the program and pressed \emph{Calculate} without changing
anything was told the mixture ratio was outside the usable range. The band was
corrected rather than loosened: the upper edge moved from 6.0 to 12.0, which
brings the optimum inside, and the lower edge moved from 0.5 to 4.0, which
means heavily fuel-rich designs such as O/F 2.5 now raise a warning where they
used to be silent. Section~\ref{sec:design} explains why a storable oxidizer
optimizes so high.
\end{warnbox}


\begin{warnbox}
\small\color{hrmadark}
\textbf{Warnings are the product, not a defect.} Rather than invent a value
for something it cannot compute, HRMA warns explicitly or says it did not
model the case. When you see an orange or red message, cross-check that
quantity by hand or with an independent code before you cut metal.
\end{warnbox}

\section{Designing a Motor from Scratch}\label{sec:design}

Section~\ref{sec:example} drove one motor through the program with the values
already chosen. This section is about choosing them. It follows a single
decision chain, from a mission statement to a motor the program will publish a
verdict on. At every step three things are named: the field you type into, the
number the program publishes back, and the band that number has to land in. Where
a band is printed here, it is the band the program itself holds; the program
prints it in the rejection or the warning, so you never have to take it from
this guide alone.

The chain is written for the hybrid page, because that page exposes the most
sizing levers. Section~\ref{sec:solidliquid} says which steps change on the
solid and liquid pages.

\subsection{Step 0: write the mission down before you open the page}

Four numbers define the operating point, and everything else in the form is a
consequence of them plus a hardware choice.

\textbf{Total impulse} is the size of the motor in one number. On the
\emph{Thrust \& Time} tab you enter \emph{Thrust (N)} and \emph{Burn Time (s)}
and the page prints the product live as \emph{Calculated Total Impulse}; on the
\emph{Total Impulse} tab you enter the impulse directly, between 1000 and
1{,}000{,}000~N$\cdot$s, and may leave thrust or burn time empty for automatic.
\textbf{Thrust}, accepted from 1 to $10^{7}$~N, fixes the throat area and with it
the whole gas path. \textbf{Burn time}, accepted 0.1 to 1000~s, fixes the grain
web and the fuel mass. \textbf{Design altitude}, entered as \emph{Altitude (m)}
between 0 and 50{,}000, fixes the ambient pressure through the US Standard
Atmosphere 1976 and with it the useful expansion ratio; the resulting
\emph{Ambient Pressure (bar)} is shown next to it.

\begin{notebox}
\small\color{hrmadark}
\textbf{Thrust and burn time are not independent of the vehicle.} The program
sizes a motor for the impulse you ask for; it does not check that the thrust is
enough to leave the rail or that the acceleration is survivable. Those are
trajectory questions, and the Launch Site page or the trajectory analysis type
is where you ask them. Decide the impulse first, then split it into thrust and
time.
\end{notebox}

\subsection{Step 1: choose the propellant pair}

The pair decides what the program can say about your motor, not just how well it
performs. Two selectors, \emph{Fuel Type} and \emph{Oxidizer Type}, drive the
thermochemistry, the regression coefficients, and which validation bands apply.

\emph{Fuel Type} offers HTPB, Polyethylene, PMMA, Paraffin Wax, ABS, PLA,
Carbon (Graphite), Aluminum Powder, and two custom entries. \emph{Oxidizer
Type} offers Nitrous Oxide, Liquid Oxygen, Hydrogen Peroxide, Compressed Air and
a custom entry. The separate oxidizer phase selector (Liquid or Gas) is a state,
not a second oxidizer: it decides how the density and the injection model are
resolved.

\textbf{What you gain by staying on a characterized pair.} The validation layer
holds published bands for a small set of pairs. Where a band exists, an
out-of-band result is named with the band printed next to it; where no band
exists, the calculation still runs but the check falls back to a generic band
and tells you less. The pairs with their own bands are
N\textsubscript{2}O/HTPB, N\textsubscript{2}O/paraffin, and, for specific
impulse only, LOX/HTPB.

\begin{center}
\footnotesize
\begin{tabular}{@{}>{\raggedright}p{3.6cm} >{\raggedright}p{3.3cm}
>{\raggedright}p{3.3cm} p{3.4cm}@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Pair} & \textbf{Isp band (s)} & \textbf{$c^*$ band (m/s)} &
\textbf{O/F band} \\
\midrule
N\textsubscript{2}O / HTPB & 180--250 & 1350--1650 & 4.0--12.0 \\
N\textsubscript{2}O / paraffin & 190--260 & 1400--1700 & 4.0--12.0 \\
LOX / HTPB & 220--280 & generic & generic \\
Any other pair & 150--300 & 1200--1800 & 0.5--8.0 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\textbf{What the program does when the fuel has no fitted regression law.} The
regression coefficients $a$ and $n$ in \emph{Fuel Configuration} are filled from
the database for the fuel you picked. For a fuel with no validated fit, the
warning reads \emph{no validated regression coefficients for this fuel}, states
that literature values for a similar fuel were used, and tells you to treat the
regression rate, the burn time and the O/F shift as indicative. That warning is
the decision point: either accept an indicative answer, or fit your own $a$ and
$n$ from static fires and type them into \emph{Fuel Configuration}. The default
pair arrives with $a = 3.68 \times 10^{-5}$ and $n = 0.555$, and the fields
accept $a$ in $10^{-6}$ to $10^{-2}$ and $n$ in 0.1 to 1.0.

\subsection{Step 2: choose the mixture ratio}

O/F is the first number that has four different answers in the program, and
knowing which one you are looking at removes most of the confusion on this page.

\begin{center}
\footnotesize
\begin{tabular}{@{}>{\raggedright}p{3.8cm} >{\raggedright}p{3.0cm} p{6.8cm}@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Number} & \textbf{Value} & \textbf{What it is} \\
\midrule
The page default & 7.5 & The tabulated theoretical optimum for the pair the two
selectors start on, N\textsubscript{2}O with HTPB. It is a starting value, not a
verdict, and it is tagged to that pair \\
The endpoint band & 0.1--50 & What the hybrid geometry endpoint will accept
before it refuses the request \\
The canonical band & 0.5--20 & The repository-wide envelope the endpoint band is
derived against \\
The validation band & 4.0--12.0 & What raises a warning after the calculation,
for this pair \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

A value inside the endpoint band but outside the validation band calculates and
then warns. That is the intended behavior: the first band asks whether the
request is physical, the second asks whether the result is inside the range the
model was checked against.

\textbf{Why the optimum sits so high for nitrous oxide.} The band's own
published basis records that N\textsubscript{2}O peaks near O/F 7 to 8 and stays
high and flat across roughly 4 to 12, because about two thirds of the impulse
comes from the exothermic decomposition of the N\textsubscript{2}O itself and
only about one third from burning the fuel with the oxygen it releases. Storable
oxidizers therefore optimize at high mixture ratio; LOX pairs optimize near 2.
If you carry an O/F habit over from a LOX engine you will design a
fuel-rich hybrid and see it in both bands at once.

\textbf{A worked reading.} On the default pair, moving O/F from 2.5 to 6.0 moves
the specific impulse from 185.4~s to 230.3~s, and an O/F of 2.5 also pushes
$c^*$ to 1331~m/s, which is under the 1350~m/s lower edge of the $c^*$ band. At
O/F 7.5 both bands are satisfied. If you need a fuel-rich motor for a reason
outside the program, such as an ablative liner budget, expect two warnings and
read them as the record of a deliberate choice rather than as a fault.

\textbf{Using \emph{Find Optimum}.} Pick both selectors first, then press
\emph{Find Optimum}. The pair is sent to the server and the optimum is computed
for it. The page prints no ``distance from optimum'' figure for an arbitrary
pair, because it does not know the optimum until it has computed it.

\subsection{Step 3: choose the chamber pressure}

Chamber pressure buys performance and is paid for twice, once in the tank and
once in the wall.

The endpoint accepts 1 to 500~bar on the hybrid page, 5 to 200 on the solid
page and 10 to 500 on the liquid page; outside those it refuses the request. The
validation check is tighter, 5 to 100~bar, and it raises a warning after the
calculation rather than blocking it.

\textbf{The tank price.} The oxidizer has to arrive at the injector above
chamber pressure, and the injector has to drop a real fraction of the chamber
pressure to stop the feed system from talking to the combustion. The program
holds that fraction as a band, and it is the same band the stability panel
judges chug with:

\begin{center}
\footnotesize
\begin{tabular}{@{}>{\raggedright}p{3.2cm} p{10.8cm}@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{$\Delta P_{\mathrm{inj}} / P_c$} & \textbf{What the program says} \\
\midrule
$\geq 0.20$ & The recommended design rule: the feed system is decoupled from
the chamber \\
0.15 to 0.20 & Marginal, the low end of the classical band \\
$< 0.15$ & Chug risk: feed-to-chamber coupling is possible \\
$> 0.35$ & Above the classical band. This ceiling is a feed-system cost
criterion, not a stability criterion: beyond it you pay tank pressure and pump
work for no stability gain \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The \emph{Pressure Drop Override (\%)} field on the injector tab accepts 10 to
50 and starts at 20. So a 20~bar chamber at the recommended ratio needs about
4~bar across the injector, and the tank has to stand above 24~bar plus the line
and valve losses before any ullage or blowdown margin. The example in
Section~\ref{sec:example} enters 50~bar, which is a self-pressurizing
N\textsubscript{2}O storage state rather than a computed minimum.

\textbf{The wall price.} Hoop stress in a thin cylinder is proportional to
$P_c D / 2t$, so doubling chamber pressure at fixed diameter doubles the
thickness the structural module will ask for, and the mass goes with it. This is
the trade the \emph{Structural Safety} panel is there to price: run it before
and after a pressure change rather than after the design is frozen.

\begin{notebox}
\small\color{hrmadark}
\textbf{If you leave chamber pressure empty.} The hybrid solver still runs, on a
declared placeholder, and the placeholder is named in the
\texttt{defaults\_applied} block. It is a placeholder so the arithmetic can
proceed, not a recommended pressure. Read the declaration block before you quote
any number from such a run.
\end{notebox}

\subsection{Step 4: choose the expansion ratio for the altitude you fly at}

\emph{Ae/At Ratio} accepts \texttt{0} for automatic, in which case the solver
sizes the nozzle for the ambient pressure that follows from the altitude you
entered. When you set it yourself, three checks act on it.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em]
  \item \textbf{The geometry band, 4 to 250.} Outside it the warning prints your
  ratio and the range. A sea-level design at modest chamber pressure frequently
  lands under 4, which is why the example motor in Section~\ref{sec:example}
  raises that warning.
  \item \textbf{The separation criterion.} The solver applies a Summerfield
  criterion: the boundary layer separates when the exit pressure falls below
  $k$ times the ambient pressure, with $k$ at 0.4 by default and an accepted
  band of 0.35 to 0.40. At sea level that puts the separation threshold near
  0.4~bar of exit pressure. Push the expansion ratio past that and the nozzle is
  no longer flowing full.
  \item \textbf{What a separated nozzle reports.} Thrust and thrust coefficient
  are then published on the separated effective expansion ratio, not on the
  geometric one, and the warning says so. The number is not wrong; it is
  answering a different question from the one your drawing asks.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{The altitude trade in one sentence.} A ratio matched to vacuum is
unusable at sea level because it separates, and a ratio matched to sea level
wastes exit area in vacuum; a first stage is sized for the pressure it spends
most of its impulse at, not for the pad. If you want to see the penalty, use the
altitude sweep analysis type, which walks the ratio you chose across the
altitude interval you set.

\begin{warnbox}
\small\color{hrmadark}
\textbf{A clamped optimum is not a matched nozzle.} On the liquid page, if the
solved optimum expansion ratio hits the edge of the accepted band, the warning
states which edge, prints the clamped value, and says the nozzle is no longer
matched to the design altitude. Treat that as a design instruction, not as a
rounding note: change the chamber pressure or the design altitude until the
optimum falls inside the band.
\end{warnbox}

\subsection{Step 5: let the throat follow from thrust}

On the hybrid and liquid pages the throat is a result, not an input. Thrust,
chamber pressure and the thrust coefficient fix the throat area through
$F = C_F P_c A_t$, and the mass flow follows from $\dot m = P_c A_t / c^*$. That
is why the program refuses a request that carries a quantity it publishes: the
throat diameter, the oxidizer mass flow and the fuel mass flow are outputs of
that chain, and accepting them as inputs would let you label the solver's own
number as your own.

The consequences are worth stating as rules, because they are the fastest way to
steer the geometry:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em]
  \item At fixed chamber pressure, thrust scales with throat area, so with the
  square of throat diameter. Halving the thrust target shrinks the throat
  diameter by about 30 percent, not by half.
  \item At fixed thrust, raising chamber pressure shrinks the throat. That is
  the mechanism behind ``high pressure makes a small engine'', and it is also
  why the wall gets thicker: the same force is carried on a smaller, more
  highly loaded shell.
  \item The canonical envelope for a throat is 1~mm to 2~m. A result outside
  that is a unit error somewhere upstream, almost always a diameter typed in
  meters into a millimeter field or the reverse.
\end{itemize}

On the solid page the throat is an input, \emph{Throat Diameter Dt (mm)},
accepted 1 to 500~mm and auto-filled after a calculation. There you set the
throat and read the chamber pressure that results from the propellant and the
burning area, which is the opposite direction of causation and the reason solid
sizing feels different.

\subsection{Step 6: size the chamber with L* and the contraction ratio}

\emph{L* Value (m)} is chamber volume divided by throat area. It sets how long
the gas stays in the chamber before it reaches the throat, and therefore how
completely it burns.

\begin{center}
\footnotesize
\begin{tabular}{@{}>{\raggedright}p{4.0cm} >{\raggedright}p{3.2cm} p{6.4cm}@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Field} & \textbf{Accepted} & \textbf{Note} \\
\midrule
L* Value (m), hybrid & from 0.1~m & No upper band is imposed: in a hybrid the
useful L* varies very widely with the grain, so an upper limit would refuse
legitimate designs. A zero or negative value is refused, because the chamber

Characteristic Length L* (m), liquid & 0.3--5, starts at 1.2 & A tighter band,

Contraction Ratio Ac/At, hybrid & 0--10, \texttt{0} is automatic & Chamber

\textbf{What a low contraction ratio does.} The warning names it directly: the
chamber Mach number rises, the injector-face pressure climbs above the chamber
pressure, and the flow approaches choking inside the chamber. At that point the
one-dimensional premise the whole page rests on is straining, and the pressure
you typed is no longer the pressure the injector sees. Raise the chamber
diameter or lower the contraction ratio target until the warning clears.

\textbf{Chamber Length Override (mm) and what it may not do.} The override
replaces the length derived from L*. If your value is shorter than the fuel
grain plus the pre-combustion chamber, it is rejected and the warning quotes
both your value and the minimum. It is not trimmed to fit, because a trimmed
length is a different motor from the one you asked for. Because the chamber
cannot be shorter than the grain it contains, the L* the motor actually realizes
can be larger than the one you requested; the program reports what it realized
rather than echoing your request.

\subsection{Step 7: size the grain and the port}

This is where a hybrid design is won or lost, and where the program has the most
to say.

Three fields drive it. \emph{Initial Port Diameter (mm)} accepts 0 to 500 with
\texttt{0} meaning automatic: a small port means high oxidizer flux and high
regression, and a shorter time to burn through. \emph{Number of Ports} accepts
one or more, and empty means one; more ports divide the flow and shorten the
grain. \emph{Mass Flux} accepts 0 to 1000~kg/m\textsuperscript{2}$\cdot$s, again
with \texttt{0} for automatic, and is the design oxidizer flux in the port.

\textbf{The oxidizer flux gate.} Port oxidizer mass flux $G_{ox}$ is the
oxidizer mass flow divided by the port area. The program warns at
600~kg/m\textsuperscript{2}$\cdot$s, which is the lower edge of the band where
flooding sets in (600 to 700); it warns early on purpose. Above it, the port
cannot take the oxidizer you are pushing through it and the regression law stops
describing the surface. The fix is more port area: a larger initial port, or
more ports.

\textbf{The slenderness gate.} The program warns when the grain or chamber
length-to-diameter ratio exceeds 10. This is a manufacturing and combustion
practice band, not a single published constant, and the warning prints the ratio
it measured. It matters more than it looks: the default hybrid inputs produce a
grain L/D of 18.9 and a chamber L/D of 19.7, which is an 80~mm tube 1.58~m long,
and at O/F 2.0 the ratio measures 26.3. Selecting four ports on the same run
brings the grain ratio down to 8.6. That is the standard cure, and it is why
multi-port grains exist.

\textbf{What multi-port modeling does and does not cover.} The warning is
explicit: area and perimeter scaling are correct, but port interaction, web
thickness between ports, and burn-through between ports are not modeled. Use the
equivalent-port result to size, then check the web between ports by hand.

\textbf{The web gate.} If the port breaks through to the case before the burn
time you asked for, the warning says the fuel web is exhausted early and states
that performance beyond that point is not physical. Three levers fix it: a
larger grain outer diameter, a smaller initial port, or a shorter burn time.

\textbf{Regression and the feedback you cannot see.} The regression law
$r = a\,G^{n}$ is evaluated against the oxidizer flux, and the port grows as it
burns, so the flux falls and the O/F shifts through the burn. The charts on the
results page plot exactly this: the regression rate with port growth, and the
mass flow rates. If the regression iteration does not converge, the warning says
the reported rate is the last iterate and not a converged solution; do not quote
that run.

\textbf{The swirl injector has two design modes and you must pick one.} Leave

says so rather than quietly preferring one of your two instructions. A swirl
chamber narrower than 1.25 times the exit orifice breaks the geometric premise
of the model and is reported.

\subsection{Step 9: choose the wall, the material and the margin}

The hybrid page accepts a \emph{Wall Thickness (mm)} of 2 to 20 and starts at 5,
with a \emph{Safety Factor} of 2 to 6 starting at 4.0. The solid page accepts a
\emph{Case Wall Thickness (mm)} of 2 to 100 and a \emph{Safety Factor FoS} of 1.1
to 10 starting at 2.5; the liquid page accepts a \emph{Chamber Wall Thickness
(mm)} of 0.2 to 150 starting at 5 and the same safety-factor band. On all three,
the thickness is the hardware and the safety factor is the target.

\textbf{The distinction that catches everyone.} Entering a safety factor does
not make the motor safe. Your real wall thickness travels with it, so the module
computes the minimum safety factor that pressure, diameter, material and
thickness actually deliver, and compares it with your target. The reported
minimum safety factor is the answer; the field is the question. A value outside
the accepted range is refused with the range named, and a value that cannot be
read as a number is refused as such, in both cases without substituting a design
value.

\textbf{The pressure ladder you are designing against.} The \emph{Pressure
Vessel} panel makes the margin chain explicit, and it is worth designing against
the chain rather than against a single number:

\begin{center}
\footnotesize
\begin{tabular}{@{}>{\raggedright}p{4.8cm} p{9.2cm}@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Rung} & \textbf{How it is set} \\
\midrule

pressure \\

\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The weld joint efficiency selector (1.00 full radiography, 0.85 spot
radiography, 0.70 none) is a real design lever here, not paperwork: it scales
the allowable directly, so an unradiographed seam costs you 30 percent of the
wall.

\begin{warnbox}
\small\color{hrmadark}
\textbf{Do not leave the vessel wall thickness blank and then read the green
badge.} With the thickness blank, the panel sizes the wall to hit its own
margin target, and the burst margin it then reports is a read-back of that
target rather than an independent verification. The panel says so in the card
tip. Type the thickness you intend to build and read the margin you actually
have.
\end{warnbox}

\textbf{The thermal verdict is a separate question, and wall thickness is not
its lever.} A first honest hybrid design almost always produces two critical
thermal messages at once: the chamber wall exceeds the melting point of its
material, and the equilibrium wall temperature is pinned within five percent
of the adiabatic wall temperature. The second explains the first. With the
cooling selector on \emph{None} the coolant-side film coefficient is natural
convection, around 25~W/m\textsuperscript{2}K, which is effectively no path
for the heat to leave; a steady-state balance with no outflow has one
solution, the wall at the gas temperature, and that exceeds any realistic
metal. Thickening the wall does not help, because thickness does not appear
in a steady-state balance at all: it changes how fast the wall arrives, not
where it stops. The levers that move this verdict, in the order worth trying:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.6em]
  \item \textbf{Select a cooling method.} On the hybrid page the
  \emph{Cooling} selector offers None, Radial Channels and Spiral Channels,
  and the selection now reaches the thermal model rather than being ignored.
  This is the only input that changes the outflow term, so it is the only one
  that can unpin the wall.
  \item \textbf{Lower the gas temperature.} Moving O/F away from the
  stoichiometric peak costs specific impulse and buys chamber temperature
  directly. A few hundred kelvin here is worth more than any amount of steel.
  \item \textbf{Change the material.} AISI~304 melts at 1673~K, which is
  below the flame temperature of every useful propellant pair. Inconel~718
  buys margin; a graphite or ablative liner changes the problem from
  conduction to sacrifice.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{And read the verdict for what it is.} A short-burn amateur motor
often survives a wall temperature this model calls fatal, because it never
reaches equilibrium: the case absorbs the burn and the firing ends first. The
program does not credit that, and says so rather than guessing your burn
duration against your wall thickness. Treat the message as a statement about
the equilibrium your design is heading toward, and reach for the transient
solver, a liner, or thermocouple data before you decide a short burn saves
you.

\section{Reading the Results}\label{sec:reading}

A calculation produces numbers, bands, declarations and, sometimes, a refusal to
give a verdict. This section is about turning that into a decision. It is
organized the way you should read a result: the performance numbers first,
because they tell you which half of the motor to look at; then the declarations,
because they tell you how much of the answer was computed; then the warnings,
because they tell you what the program will not stand behind.

\subsection{Three performance numbers, and what each one blames}

Specific impulse is the product of the other two:
$I_{sp} = c^{*} C_F / g_0$. That factorization is the single most useful
diagnostic on the page, because the two factors describe different hardware.

\begin{center}
\footnotesize
\begin{tabular}{@{}>{\raggedright}p{3.4cm} >{\raggedright}p{4.6cm} p{6.0cm}@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Quantity} & \textbf{What it measures} & \textbf{What an error in it
means} \\
\midrule
$c^*$, characteristic velocity & The combustion side only. It depends on the
propellants, the mixture ratio and the chamber conditions, and not at all on the
nozzle & Thermochemistry, the propellant record, the mixture ratio, or the
combustion efficiency you supplied \\
$C_F$, thrust coefficient & The nozzle side only: how well the chamber gas is
turned into axial momentum & Expansion ratio, contour, divergence, separation,
or the ambient pressure you designed against \\
$I_{sp}$, specific impulse & The two multiplied, per unit weight of propellant,
referenced to the standard $g_0$ & Read it only after you know which factor
moved \\
Total impulse & Thrust integrated over the burn & The size of the motor, not its
quality \\
$L^{*}$, characteristic length & Chamber volume over throat area, so residence
time & Too short and the gas leaves before it finishes burning, which shows up
as a $c^*$ shortfall, not a nozzle problem \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\textbf{The diagnostic in practice.} If $c^*$ is inside its band and $I_{sp}$ is
low, stop looking at the propellant: the loss is in the nozzle, and the
candidates in order of likelihood are a low expansion ratio, a separation call,
and divergence loss on a conical contour. If $c^*$ is below its band, the nozzle
is irrelevant until you have fixed the chamber: check the propellant pair, the
mixture ratio, and whether you supplied a combustion efficiency.

\textbf{How the program decides between a warning and a critical message.} The
severity is computed from how far outside the band you are, not from the wording:
a specific impulse below 80 percent of the band's lower edge or above 120 percent
of its upper edge is critical, and so is a $c^*$ below 90 percent of its lower
edge. Inside those factors, an out-of-band value is a warning. This is why a
warning and a critical message about the same quantity are not the same
statement: the first says the result is outside the checked range, the second
says it is outside anything the model can defend.

\textbf{Combustion efficiency scales everything downstream.} If you supply a
$c^*$ efficiency on the liquid page (\emph{Combustion Efficiency} $\eta_c$,
50 to 100 percent, starts at 97) or on the solid page (0.5 to 1, starts at
0.95), the delivered $c^*$ is that factor times the theoretical value, and
the factor then propagates into specific impulse, mass flow and throat area.
Measured hybrid efficiencies are typically 0.90 to 0.97. The solver accepts
a wider physical band, 0.5 to 1.05, and applies a value outside even that
band as given rather than silently correcting it, while recording that it
was out of band: an efficiency of 0.300 takes a theoretical $c^*$ of
1328.19~m/s down to a delivered 398.46~m/s, and every number downstream
moves with it.

\textbf{The hybrid page has no such field.} The hybrid solver accepts a
$c^*$ efficiency only through the Python API and through the Uncertainty
analysis, so on the hybrid page the reported $c^*$ is the theoretical
equilibrium value and the 0.300 figure above cannot be produced from the
interface at all. This matters when you compare a hybrid result with a
liquid one: the hybrid number is an upper bound that no real motor reaches,
while the liquid number already carries a 97 percent derate. Subtract
roughly three to ten percent from a hybrid $c^*$ before putting it next to a
delivered figure from a test stand, and if a hybrid result looks impossibly
high, this is the first thing to suspect.

\subsection{Reading the flow regime}

The \emph{Nozzle Flow} panel classifies the nozzle into one of six regimes, and
the label is a design instruction rather than a description:

\midrule

of the bracket means the chemistry has no time to recombine in the nozzle, which
is a residence-time statement about your contour.

Hybrid $c^*$ & bias $-0.1$\%, median APE 2.3\%, $N = 18$ & Use it. A few percent
is below the batch-to-batch scatter of real propellant \\
Hybrid specific impulse & bias $+8.2$\%, median APE 7.7\%, $N = 18$ & Optimistic

\end{center}

\textbf{The regression-rate row hides two populations, and which one you are in
decides everything.} Split by source on the published run: the paraffin with
gaseous oxygen records ($N = 17$) sit on their own published law with a bias of
$+0.4$ percent and a median APE of 4.7 percent, while the low-flux HTPB with
N\textsubscript{2}O records ($N = 18$) are under-predicted by roughly a factor of
two, bias $-48.7$ percent and median APE 49.3 percent. The cause is documented
rather than accidental: a single published pair of coefficients per fuel, fitted
in a flux range of roughly 10 to 30~g/cm\textsuperscript{2}$\cdot$s, is being
evaluated below that range where radiation and small-motor effects dominate. No
coefficient was tuned to hide it.

\textbf{The practical rule.} If your port flux is low, expect the program to
under-predict regression, which means it will over-predict burn time and
under-predict fuel flow, which shifts O/F oxidizer-rich in reality relative to
the prediction. If regression rate drives your design, fit $a$ and $n$ from your
own static fires and enter them in \emph{Fuel Configuration}. That is the single
highest-value input you can give this program.

\subsection{The declaration chain: reading a number's history}

Every published value carries where it came from. Reading that chain takes three
questions, in order.

\textbf{Question 1: was it computed, or assumed?} The value source legend in
every Analysis Dock panel tags each input:

\texttt{COMPUTED} means it came from the solver run you just did,
\texttt{USER} that you typed it, \texttt{ASSUMED} that the panel applied a
default which is then listed by name in the ``assumed on your behalf'' block, and
\texttt{NOT MODELLED} that there is no model for it here, so no number was
produced.

The ledger below each panel counts the tags, so you can see at a glance how much
of a result rests on assumptions. A result built mostly from \texttt{ASSUMED}
inputs is a screening number.

\textbf{Question 2: what basis was it judged on?} Wherever a number could be
mistaken for a measurement or for a different quantity, a basis field travels
with it and says which. Four examples you will meet:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em]
  \item The predicted burst pressure carries a basis stating that it is the
  smaller of the Faupel thick-wall result and the thin-wall plastic limit. It is
  a model output. Nothing was burst.
  \item The specific-heat ratio in the thermal chain carries its source. Leave
  the field empty and the result says the repository default 1.20 was used, with
  \texttt{gamma\_source: repo\_default}.
  \item The injector pressure-drop band carries the origin of each edge: the
  lower edge is the chug stability minimum, the upper edge is a feed-system cost
  criterion. They are different kinds of limit and the report says so.
  \item The proof pressure carries the code factor it came from, so a number
  computed under AIAA S-080 is not confused with one computed under ASME VIII.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Question 3: was the verdict earned?} A number and a verdict are separate
products, and the program will publish the first while withholding the second.
The structural module makes this explicit: supply your real wall thickness and
it runs in verification mode, so the reported minimum safety factor is what your
hardware delivers. Leave the thickness out and a sizing routine picks a
thickness that meets the target, after which the reported margin reads back that
target. Both cases print a number; only one of them is evidence.

\begin{notebox}
\small\color{hrmadark}
\textbf{The test for an earned green.} Ask what would have had to be different
for this badge to be red. If the answer is ``nothing, the sizer aimed at exactly
this'', the badge is a read-back. The panels that can produce a read-back say so
in the card tip rather than leaving you to work it out.
\end{notebox}

\subsection{The four ways the program declines to answer}

They are not interchangeable, and the distinction tells you what to do next.

\begin{center}
\footnotesize
\begin{tabular}{@{}>{\raggedright}p{4.0cm} >{\raggedright}p{4.4cm} p{5.6cm}@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{What you see} & \textbf{What it means} & \textbf{What to do} \\
\midrule
\texttt{NOT MODELLED} & There is no model here for this quantity in this
configuration & Nothing in the program will produce it. Compute it elsewhere \\
\texttt{NOT EVALUATED} & There is a model, but it was not run because the load
case or the input it needs was not declared & Supply the missing declaration if
the form exposes it; otherwise expect this state \\
``No verdict published'' & The number exists and is shown, but the
self-consistency gate that would justify a verdict does not cover this run &
Read the field data, do not read a pass or a fail into it \\
``No published data'' & There is no citable source for the constant the model
would need & Change the material or the propellant, or supply the constant if
you have a source \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

None of these is a defect. Each one is the program declining to produce a number
that would not survive being questioned. The list of what this version
deliberately withholds is in Section~\ref{sec:scope}, and it is worth reading
before you plan an analysis around a verdict that will not arrive.

\subsection{Reading the two-phase status on a solid motor}

This is the one verdict to check before quoting a solid specific impulse, and it
has three distinct states rather than two.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em]
  \item \textbf{The \emph{Two-Phase Loss Factor} field is empty by default}
  (it accepts 0.8 to 1). Left empty, the solver applies no user two-phase
  factor and declares that it did not.
  \item \textbf{The published specific impulse therefore carries no verified
  two-phase correction.} The status appears in the API response, in the band
  under the specific-impulse cards, in the PDF report, and in the comment header
  of the \texttt{.eng} file, so the qualification travels with the number into
  whatever flight simulator you hand it to.
  \item \textbf{If the solver publishes no status at all}, the band says
  \emph{not published by the solver} rather than assuming the correction was
  applied or was unnecessary.
\end{itemize}

The module also carries its own linear two-phase loss estimate. It is a
diagnostic: reported, never applied. Outside the particle mass fraction window
the anchoring reference covers, it is withheld with the reason rather than
extrapolated. KN-sugar propellants and black powder fall outside that window;
APCP and double base fall inside it. Seeing the diagnostic withheld on a sugar
propellant is the model refusing to extrapolate, not a missing feature.

\subsection{When two numbers on the same screen disagree}

Several quantities appear twice with different meanings, and each pair is
explained where it occurs. The heat-map legend on the 3D model is evaluated at a
reference cooled wall, which is a design load, while the thermal panel's wall
temperature is an equilibrium result (Section~\ref{sec:example}). The vacuum
against sea-level specific impulse gap and the values the liquid solver computes
for itself are both covered with the liquid verdicts
(Section~\ref{sec:solidliquid}). Web thickness and case free volume are derived
on the solid page, so a contradicting entry is quoted back against the geometric
value. And the predicted burst pressure is the smaller of two correlations, not a
test result.

In every one of these cases the two numbers are answering different questions,
and the screen says which. If a pair looks contradictory, the fastest resolution
is to read the basis line under the number rather than to recompute.

\begin{warnbox}
\small\color{hrmadark}
\textbf{One reading habit worth building.} Read the Warnings panel, then the
declaration blocks, then the headline numbers. Read in the other order and you
will anchor on a figure before you know how much of it was assumed. Every
mechanism in this program is designed so that the qualification of a number
reaches you at the same time as the number; the only way to lose it is to skip
past it.
\end{warnbox}

\section{The Analysis Dock and the Analysis Center}\label{sec:dock}

Below the results, two containers hold the deeper analyses. The
\textbf{Analysis Dock} holds the engineering panels that run on the motor
you just calculated. The \textbf{Analysis Center} holds the heavier solves,
organized as a component-by-analysis matrix.

\fig{img/en-06-deck.png}{The Analysis Dock: the category tabs, a panel's
pre-filled inputs and the value-source legend. Result plots appear in the
panel once you run that analysis.}

\subsection{How the Dock works}

The Dock describes itself on screen: it runs detailed engineering analyses
on the current motor design, its inputs are pre-filled from the latest
calculation results, you can override any value before running, and the
request is always built from the form fields shown. Panels are grouped by
category (\texttt{FLOW}, \texttt{THERMAL}, \texttt{STRUCTURAL},
\texttt{PRESSURE VESSEL}, \texttt{FEED SYSTEM}, \texttt{SAFETY},
\texttt{PERFORMANCE}, \texttt{UNCERTAINTY}, \texttt{VALIDATION},
\texttt{CORRELATION}, \texttt{COMPARE}) and you can lay them out as
\emph{Tabs} or as a \emph{Grid}.

Three mechanisms appear in every panel and are worth learning once:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em]
  \item \textbf{The value source legend.} Each input carries a source tag:
  \texttt{COMPUTED} (it came from the solver run), \texttt{USER} (you typed
  it), \texttt{ASSUMED} (the panel applied a default, which is listed by
  name), or \texttt{NOT MODELLED}. The ledger below the panel counts them,
  so you can see at a glance how much of a result rests on assumptions.
  \item \textbf{The ``What changed'' block.} After a second run, the panel
  reports which values moved and by how much relative to the previous run,
  and says plainly when nothing changed.
  \item \textbf{Refusal to run on blanks.} If a required field is empty, the
  panel does not send the request. It lists the empty fields and states that
  a blank field is not a value.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{The panels, one by one}

\textbf{Nozzle Flow} (\texttt{FLOW}) solves the quasi-1D compressible flow:
regime, $P(x)$, $M(x)$ and $C_F$. Its six required fields are born empty. If
you press \emph{Analyze} on a cold panel it still answers, but every default
it applied appears by name in the assumed-on-your-behalf block. Arriving
from a motor page fills the fields with that motor's real values.

\textbf{Advanced Performance} (\texttt{PERFORMANCE}) draws the 3D
performance surface, the Mach contour and the heat-flux figure. The relations
behind these figures are stated in the last section of the Formulas page.

\textbf{Structural Safety} (\texttt{STRUCTURAL}) covers the pressure vessel,
buckling and fatigue. Inputs: chamber diameter, wall thickness, material,
pressure, design factor. Two things about its verdicts:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em]
  \item \textbf{Buckling is not declared safe unless it was evaluated
  against a load.} The case buckling block used to compute the axial mode
  only and report a pass without ever comparing the external-pressure mode
  with an applied pressure. Unless an external-pressure load case is
  declared, \texttt{buckling\_status} now reads \texttt{NOT\_EVALUATED}, and
  the API response carries a \texttt{buckling\_status\_scope} field naming
  the modes that were not covered at all. That field is published in the
  response body, not printed on the panel, and the load case itself is
  accepted only by \texttt{/api/advanced-analysis}, never by this panel's own
  endpoint. No page sends it, so on every screen the not-evaluated state is
  what you get. The panel reference in Section~\ref{sec:dock} gives the exact
  keys and what to do about them.
  \item \textbf{``Actual burst pressure'' is a prediction, not a
  measurement.} The number is the smaller of the Faupel thick-wall and the
  thin-wall membrane limits. No hydrostatic burst test stands behind it. The
  same value is published as a predicted burst pressure and the basis field
  says so.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Pressure Vessel} (\texttt{PRESSURE VESSEL}) sizes the vessel and
reports the maximum allowable working pressure alongside the burst
prediction described above.

\textbf{Bolted Joint} (\texttt{STRUCTURAL}) works the Shigley chain:
preload, tightening torque, and the separation margin of the joint.

\textbf{Thermal Safety} (\texttt{THERMAL}) runs the Bartz heat-transfer
chain and reports wall temperatures. If the thermal path did not run, the
wall temperature reads \texttt{NOT EVALUATED} instead of showing a stale or
assumed number. The ratio of specific heats is checked rather than corrected:
a value outside the band the frozen Bartz relations are defined on
(1.05--1.67) is refused with the band named. Supply no gamma and the
repository default is used, but the result now says that it was a default. The specific-heat ratio ($\gamma$) input is accepted in the
1.05--1.67 band; a value outside it is rejected, not clamped. If you leave it
empty the repository default 1.20 is used and the result carries
\texttt{gamma\_source: repo\_default}.

\textbf{Regenerative Cooling} (\texttt{THERMAL}) marches the coolant along
the wall station by station (Bartz on the gas side, Dittus-Boelter on the
coolant side). If you leave the coolant inlet temperature at the generic
300~K form default while running a cryogenic fuel, the panel substitutes the
cryogenic pump-discharge default and tells you it did.

\textbf{Thermal Protection} (\texttt{THERMAL}) covers ablative, heat-sink and
radiation-cooled walls. Its ablation chain was rebuilt for this release: the
blowing blockage is now solved self-consistently from the mass-transfer
number instead of being held at a constant that belongs to a different flow
regime, and its derivation is published next to the result. When the
solution finds no net heating, the panel does \emph{not} publish a 0.0~mm
liner as ``sized'': zero recession is not zero thickness, because the liner
is then governed by a case and bond-line temperature limit that this level
of model does not cover, and the reason is stated.

\textbf{Feed System} (\texttt{FEED SYSTEM}) covers slosh, pressurant sizing
and water hammer. The water-hammer fluid map covers seven propellants, each
with primary-source citations for bulk modulus, density and vapor pressure.
Hydrogen peroxide is deliberately absent: no citable vapor pressure was
obtained, so no water-hammer block is produced for it and no number is
invented. Blowdown pressurant sizing applies the real-gas compressibility
factor, which matters at high storage pressure. If the water-hammer peak
exceeds the pipe hoop yield pressure, the warning names both numbers and
tells you to slow the valve closure or upgrade the line.

\textbf{Comprehensive Safety} (\texttt{SAFETY}) produces the hazard picture:
explosive, toxic and fire hazards, required protective equipment, emergency
response and operational safety. Note the panel's own scope statement: the
software does not evaluate regulatory compliance, and NFPA, OSHA, DOT and
local requirements have to be assessed by a qualified safety authority for
your specific site, propellant and operation.

\textbf{Uncertainty Quantification} (\texttt{UNCERTAINTY}) runs a Monte
Carlo sweep and reports confidence bands rather than a single number.

\textbf{User Data Validation} (\texttt{VALIDATION}) compares your own
static-fire CSV against the HRMA prediction. Three things to know:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em]
  \item The relative difference is measured against \emph{your measurement}:
  the published convention is (prediction minus measurement) divided by
  measurement, and the response states which quantity is the reference so
  you read it from the data rather than from memory.
  \item \textbf{Only scored quantities are colored.} The endpoint scores the
  impulse difference and the normalized RMS error; peak thrust, mean thrust
  and burn time are not scored and are shown without a color. If the
  endpoint sends no band, no card is colored: the panel does not invent
  thresholds.
  \item An uploaded file is accepted up to 100{,}000 rows. A longer file is
  not silently truncated; the upload is refused and the row band is named.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Experimental Correlation} (\texttt{CORRELATION}) compares the model
against the built-in static-fire database, which is the same machinery
behind the validation figures in Section~\ref{sec:scope}.

\textbf{Comparative Analysis} (\texttt{COMPARE}) snapshots the current
configuration so you can put several design points side by side.


\subsection{Panel reference: what goes in, what comes out, how to read it}

The descriptions above cover each panel's character. This reference covers its
mechanics: the inputs it asks for, the numbers it publishes, and the rule for
turning those numbers into a decision. Inputs arrive pre-filled from the last
calculation wherever the panel can map them; anything you override is tagged
\texttt{USER} in the ledger.

\subsubsection{Nozzle Flow (FLOW)}

\textbf{Reads.} Chamber Pressure (bar), Chamber Temperature (K), Gamma
(frozen), Molecular Weight (g/mol), Throat Diameter (m), Exit Diameter (m),
Ambient Pressure (Pa), Axial Stations (30--60). A \emph{Fidelity Level}
selector (Fast Screening, Engineering) and a \emph{Kinetic Model} selector
(Fast equilibrium reference, Engineering JANNAF-style); the kinetic branch also
reads O/F Ratio, Fuel and Oxidizer.

\textbf{Publishes.} The flow regime label, $P(x)$ and $M(x)$ along the axis,
exit Mach, mass flow, $c^*$, thrust and effective thrust, $C_F$ with its ideal
and effective values, specific impulse bracketed between frozen and shifting,
and the kinetic loss with its band.

\textbf{Reading rule.} Start with the regime label, because it decides whether
the rest is a design point or a diagnostic. Then read the gap between
\texttt{CF IDEAL} and \texttt{CF}: that is your total nozzle loss budget. Then
read \texttt{CF EFFECTIVE} against \texttt{CF}: any gap there is separation.

\textbf{Refuses.} Nothing is sent while a required field is blank; the panel
lists the empty fields instead. Its six required fields are born empty, so a
cold run applies defaults and names every one of them.

\subsubsection{Advanced Performance (PERFORMANCE)}

\textbf{Reads.} Chamber Pressure (bar), Optimal O/F Ratio, Base Isp (s),
Expansion Ratio, Throat Area (m\textsuperscript{2}), Nozzle Length (m), Chamber
Length (m), Burn Time (s), Base Heat Flux (W/m\textsuperscript{2}), Critical
Heat Flux (MW/m\textsuperscript{2}), Chamber Temperature (K), Gamma (frozen),
Total Mass Flow (kg/s), Chamber Diameter (m).

\textbf{Publishes.} The performance surface in three dimensions, the Mach
contour, and the heat-flux figure.

\textbf{Reading rule.} This panel answers ``where on the ridge am I'' rather
than ``what is my number''. Read the surface for the gradient: a design sitting
on a steep face is sensitive to the parameter along that axis, and a design on a
plateau is not. The relations behind the three figures are stated in the last
section of the Formulas page.

\subsubsection{Structural Safety (STRUCTURAL)}

\textbf{Reads.} Chamber Pressure (bar), Chamber Diameter (m), Chamber Length
(m), Throat Diameter (m), Burn Time (s), Thrust (N), Wall Thickness (m),
Material, and three optional thermal inputs: Gas Temperature (K, blank skips the
thermal terms), hot-side and cold-side wall temperature.

\textbf{Publishes.} Sections titled \emph{Basis of this verdict}, \emph{Wall
temperature behind the thermal terms}, \emph{Wall Stresses}, \emph{Safety
Factors}, \emph{Wall Thickness}, \emph{Buckling (NASA SP-8007)}, \emph{Fatigue},
and \emph{Fasteners \& End Cap}

\textbf{Reading rule.} Read \emph{Basis of this verdict} before the verdict.
It says whether the thermal terms were included at all, which changes the stress
state materially: leaving the gas temperature blank gives you a
pressure-only answer, which is not conservative for a thin hot wall.

\textbf{Withholds.} \texttt{buckling\_status} reads \texttt{NOT\_EVALUATED}
unless an external-pressure load case is declared, and the API response carries
a \texttt{buckling\_status\_scope} field naming the modes that were not covered
at all (local and panel buckling, stiffened-shell and cutout effects, combined
bending plus pressure). That sentence is published in the response body, not
printed on the panel. The load case is accepted only by the
\texttt{POST /api/advanced-analysis} endpoint
(\texttt{analysis\_types: ["structural"]}), passed inside \texttt{motor\_data}
as \texttt{external\_pressure} and \texttt{internal\_pressure\_min} in pascals.
The Structural Safety panel endpoint does not read these two keys, and no page
sends them, so on every screen the not-evaluated state is what you get.

\textbf{What that means in practice.} A motor case that is safe against
internal pressure can still collapse under external pressure, and a hybrid or
liquid stage sees exactly that load during tank drawdown and, on a high flyer,
from aerodynamic pressure on an unpressurized section. The program is not
telling you the case is safe in that mode; it is telling you it never looked.
If your vehicle has an unpressurized section, size that mode yourself against
NASA SP-8007 rather than reading the pass on this panel as coverage.

\subsubsection{Pressure Vessel (PRESSURE VESSEL)}

\textbf{Reads.} Design Code (AIAA S-080 for flight hardware, ASME VIII Div 1
for ground and test), MEOP (bar), Inner Diameter (mm), Wall Thickness (mm, blank
auto-sizes), Material, Design Temperature (K), Weld Joint Efficiency (UW-12:
1.00 full radiography, 0.85 spot, 0.70 none), Head Type (2:1 Ellipsoidal,
Torispherical, Hemispherical).

\textbf{Publishes.} Predicted burst pressure, burst margin, required thickness,
wall used, MAWP, the full pressure ladder, head thicknesses under ASME UG-32
geometry, and the temperature derating.

\textbf{Reading rule.} Read MAWP against your MEOP first: if MAWP is below
MEOP the vessel is not qualified for the pressure you plan to run, and the card
turns red on exactly that comparison. Then read the thickness margin, which is
the ratio of the wall used to the wall required.

\textbf{Watch for.} With the wall thickness blank the vessel is sized to hit the
margin it then reports, so the margin is a read-back of the sizing target. The
card tip says so. Negative verdicts are still printed in the auto-sized case,
because a failure there is real information.

\subsubsection{Bolted Joint (STRUCTURAL)}

\textbf{Reads.} Chamber Pressure (bar), Seal / Effective Diameter (mm), Bolt
Count, Bolt Size, Property Class, Grip Length (mm), Member Material, Thread
Condition, Connection Type, and an optional Extra Axial Load (N).

\textbf{Publishes.} Recommended torque, preload $F_i$, proof safety factor,
overload factor $n_L$, separation factor $n_0$, plus the load-sharing breakdown
and the bolt data.

\textbf{Reading rule.} The separation factor is the one that decides whether the
joint leaks: it is the margin before the members come apart under pressure, and a
joint can have an acceptable proof factor and still separate. Read $n_0$ first,
then $n_L$, then the proof factor.

\textbf{Note.} The member material is required; the endpoint will not assume one
for you.

\subsubsection{Thermal Safety (THERMAL)}

\textbf{Reads.} Chamber Pressure (bar), Chamber Temperature (K), Chamber
Diameter (m), Chamber Length (m), Burn Time (s), Total Mass Flow (kg/s), Wall
Thickness (m), Wall Material, Cooling Type.

\textbf{Publishes.} $h_g$ on the gas side, the throat and chamber heat fluxes
at a reference cooled wall, the inner and outer wall temperatures, the adiabatic
wall temperature, an axial profile from chamber to nozzle exit, a heat load and
cooling assessment, and the thermal safety factors.

\textbf{Reading rule.} The adiabatic wall temperature is the ceiling the wall
would reach with no cooling at all, so it bounds the problem. The inner wall
temperature is the number to compare against the material limit. The two heat
fluxes are design loads evaluated at a reference cooled wall, which is why they
can exceed the flux at the equilibrium wall temperature.

\textbf{Refuses.} A specific-heat ratio outside 1.05--1.67 is rejected with the
band named, not clamped, because the frozen Bartz relations are not defined
outside it. If the thermal path did not run, the wall temperature reads
\texttt{NOT EVALUATED} instead of a stale number.

\subsubsection{Regenerative Cooling (THERMAL)}

\textbf{Reads.} Chamber Pressure (bar), Chamber Temperature (K), Throat
Diameter (m), Expansion Ratio, Gamma (frozen), Molecular Weight (g/mol),
Coolant, Coolant Mass Flow (kg/s), Coolant Inlet T (K), Coolant Inlet P (bar),
Number of Channels, Channel Width (mm), Channel Height (mm), Liner Thickness
(mm), Liner Material, Flow Direction.

\textbf{Publishes.} Peak wall temperature, wall margin, coolant exit
temperature, coolant temperature rise, pressure drop, peak heat flux from the
coupled wall balance, maximum coolant velocity, minimum Reynolds number.

\textbf{Reading rule.} Four numbers have to pass together and they trade
against each other. Wall margin says the liner survives; coolant temperature
rise says the coolant does not boil; pressure drop says the pump can deliver it;
minimum Reynolds says the Dittus-Boelter correlation is still valid. Narrowing
the channels improves the first and worsens the third.

\textbf{Watch for.} Leaving the coolant inlet temperature at the generic 300~K
form default with a cryogenic fuel makes the panel substitute the cryogenic
pump-discharge default and tell you it did.

\subsubsection{Thermal Protection (THERMAL)}

\textbf{Reads.} An \emph{Analysis Mode} selector, then the fields for that mode.
Ablative: net heat flux (W/m\textsuperscript{2}), ablative material, design
margin, an optional $Q^{*}$ override, burn time. Heat sink: $h_{gas}$ from
Bartz, recovery temperature, wall thickness, wall material, initial temperature,
burn time. Radiation cooled: $h_{gas}$, recovery temperature, extension
material, an optional emissivity override.

\textbf{Publishes.} Ablative: required liner thickness, total recession, mean
recession rate, the $Q^{*}$ used. Heat sink: inner and outer wall temperature at
end of burn, service limit, time to limit, margin to limit. Radiation cooled:
equilibrium wall temperature, margin, radiated flux.

\textbf{Reading rule.} In heat-sink mode, \emph{time to limit} against your burn
time is the whole verdict: a wall that reaches its service limit before burnout
has no margin no matter how good the end-of-burn temperature looks. In ablative
mode, compare the recession against the thickness you can actually machine.

\textbf{Withholds.} When the solution finds no net heating, no liner thickness
is published. Zero recession is not zero thickness: the liner is then governed by
a case and bond-line temperature limit that this level of model does not cover,
and the reason is stated.

\subsubsection{Feed System (FEED SYSTEM)}

Three analyses share one panel and are selected by tab: \emph{Slosh},
\emph{Pressurant}, \emph{Water Hammer}.

\textbf{Reads.} Slosh: tank radius, fill height, axial acceleration, propellant
density, baffle width and depth ratios. Pressurant: architecture (regulated or
blowdown), pressurant gas, propellant volume, gas temperature, and then either
tank pressure, storage pressure, regulator margin and collapse factor for the
regulated branch, or initial ullage volume, initial pressure and polytropic
exponent for the blowdown branch. Water hammer: line fluid, line length, line
inner diameter, wall thickness, working pressure and its reference, mass flow,
valve closure time, pipe material.

\textbf{Publishes.} Slosh: first-mode frequency, slosh mass fraction and mass,
pendulum length, fill ratio, and ring-baffle damping. Pressurant: delivered gas
with isothermal and adiabatic bounds, bottle volume and count, residual gas,
and for blowdown the ratio, initial and final pressure, final temperature and
trapped gas. Water hammer: the surge peak against the pipe hoop yield pressure.

\textbf{Reading rule.} In the pressurant tab the isothermal and adiabatic
results bracket reality; size the bottle on the pessimistic bound rather than on
the mean. In the water hammer tab, if the peak exceeds the pipe hoop yield
pressure the warning names both numbers, and there are exactly two cures: slow
the valve closure or upgrade the line.

\textbf{Withholds.} The water-hammer fluid map covers seven propellants with
primary-source citations for bulk modulus, density and vapor pressure. Hydrogen
peroxide is deliberately absent because no citable vapor pressure was obtained,
so no water-hammer block is produced for it.

\subsubsection{Comprehensive Safety (SAFETY)}

\textbf{Publishes.} The pressure-vessel summary as a pressure ladder
(operating, design, proof test, hydrostatic test, burst target), individual
risks scored 1 to 5, a 5 by 5 risk matrix, a mitigation priority list, a
\emph{Regulatory compliance} section, and a block listing the inputs the
endpoint filled from its own defaults.

\textbf{Reading rule.} Read the defaults block first: on this panel more than
any other, a risk score computed from assumed inputs is a category statement
rather than a site assessment. The mitigation priority list is the actionable
output.

\textbf{Scope.} The panel states its own limit: the software does not evaluate
regulatory compliance. NFPA, OSHA, DOT and local requirements have to be
assessed by a qualified safety authority for your specific site, propellant and
operation.

\subsubsection{Uncertainty Quantification (UNCERTAINTY)}

\textbf{Publishes.} A run summary carrying the confidence level, the number of
samples, the number of failed samples, the random seed, the elapsed time, the
sampler and the core used, followed by the output distributions and the
sensitivity ranking.

\textbf{Reading rule.} Read the failed-sample count before the bands. Samples
fail when the solver could not complete for that draw, and a high failure count
means the bands were computed on a censored population. Then read the
sensitivity ranking rather than the bands: it tells you which input to measure
better, which is the only action a confidence band supports.

\textbf{The nominal is not the centre of the band, and that is deliberate.}
The panel pins each nominal to the value the deterministic \emph{Calculate}
run used, so the two screens agree. Several input distributions are centered
somewhere else: the hybrid $c^*$ efficiency, for instance, is sampled from a
truncated normal with mean 0.93 on $[0.80, 1.00]$, while the nominal is
pinned at 1.0 because the main hybrid route applies no efficiency at all.
The gap is published rather than hidden, as
\texttt{mean\_shift\_percent} per output. Read it: a $-7$ percent shift on
specific impulse is not noise and not an error, it is the panel telling you
that the deterministic number on the main page is the optimistic end of the
distribution, and that the honest expectation is seven percent below the
headline. Where you see a large negative shift, quote the shifted figure in
a design review, not the headline.

\textbf{Note.} The seed is published so the run is reproducible. Quote it when
you quote a band.

\subsubsection{User Data Validation (VALIDATION)}

\textbf{Reads.} Your static-fire CSV.

\textbf{Publishes.} The thrust curve with your test data against the HRMA
prediction, a parsed-data summary, and the agreement cards: total impulse,
impulse difference, peak thrust, mean thrust, burn time, RMSE and NRMSE.

\textbf{Reading rule.} Only the impulse difference and the normalized RMS error
are scored, so only those two are colored. Peak thrust, mean thrust and burn time
are shown uncolored because there is no threshold behind them. The relative
difference is measured against your measurement: prediction minus measurement,
divided by measurement, and the response states which quantity is the reference.

\textbf{Refuses.} A file longer than 100{,}000 rows is refused with the row band
named rather than silently truncated.

\subsubsection{Experimental Correlation (CORRELATION) and Comparative Analysis (COMPARE)}

\emph{Experimental Correlation} runs the model against the built-in static-fire
database and prints the main layer as a motor-type by quantity matrix, the same
machinery behind the validation figures in Section~\ref{sec:scope} and subject to
the same three cautions. \emph{Comparative Analysis} snapshots the current
configuration and highlights the best thrust, specific impulse and efficiency
across the snapshots: it is the tool for the trade study the design chapter keeps
asking for, so change one parameter, calculate, snapshot, repeat.

\subsubsection{The nozzle CFD run card}

The Analysis Center rows and their verdicts are covered in the four subsections
below. Only the CFD run card needs its inputs listed, because it is the one card
whose settings change what the solve can be asked to prove.

It reads the chamber stagnation pressure and temperature, the ratio of specific
heats, the specific gas constant, the ambient pressure, an optional back
pressure, an optional Summerfield $k$, the grid resolution (Coarse or Standard),
the wall model (inviscid, viscous isothermal, viscous adiabatic), the turbulence
model (none for laminar, or Spalart-Allmaras in its negative variant), and, for
an isothermal wall, the wall temperature. Its result sections cover the inlet
state, the measured against expected comparison, the convergence criteria, grid
quality, transport, the boundary flux budget, the viscous cost, friction, and the
declarations. Read them in that order: a converged solve on a bad grid is not a
result.

\subsection{Reading the CFD verdict}

The nozzle CFD run prints a row of badges. They are worth reading in order,
because they tell you whether the verdict above them was earned:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em]
  \item \textbf{Convergence} is a three-valued verdict. \texttt{CONVERGED}
  with an iteration count, \texttt{NOT CONVERGED} with the residual, or
  \textbf{``no verdict published''} when the run falls outside the scope of
  the self-consistency gate. A residual plateau is called out separately: if
  the scheme residual stood still for many iterations, a bigger iteration
  budget does not earn the verdict, and the badge says so.
  \item \textbf{Separation} is judged only when the criterion applies. You
  may see a separation prediction with the fraction of the divergent length
  affected, no separation with the margin, ``separation criterion not
  applicable'', or an explicit statement that the bridge refused the
  solution or that the judgment is suspect because the field it was applied
  to did not settle.
  \item \textbf{Inviscid or viscous.} An Euler run states that it has no
  wall shear and no wall heat flux. A viscous run names the turbulence model
  and the wall treatment, and its energy budget badge says whether the wall
  heat and viscous boundary fluxes are inside the budget.
  \item \textbf{Wall resolution.} The first-cell $y^+$ badge tells you
  whether the wall is resolved, in the buffer layer, or in the log layer;
  in the last case the badge states that the wall heat flux reads low.
\end{itemize}

Only a firm verdict is shown in green. A run outside the scope of the
self-consistency check therefore never receives a positive verdict; the
field data are still published, only the verdict is withheld.

\subsection{Acoustics and combustion stability}

The \emph{Acoustic mode table} reports nozzle damping and the total loss
budget per mode. This path publishes no stability verdict, only damping
numbers against thresholds, and it says so with its own badge. The numbers
are shown in a neutral color precisely because there is no threshold behind
them to earn a color.

The \emph{Combustion stability} (chug) panel needs the feed-line length to
include the inertance term. Measured example (25~kN LOX/RP-1, 60~bar,
O/F 2.2): without a feed-line length the loop reports ``unstable'' at
77.4 Hz; with a 2.5~m line it reports ``stable'' at 33.6 Hz. One input
changes the verdict, so enter the length when you know it.

The \emph{Combustion stability} row runs the feed-coupled chug loop, and one
input changes the physics rather than the precision:

\begin{notebox}
\small\color{hrmadark}
\textbf{The feed-line length changes the model, not just the answer.}
Without a feed-line length the loop stays in the form without inertance and
declares that in its basis field. Supply a length and the inertial term
enters. On one measured liquid stage the same engine reads \emph{unstable}
with no line length and \emph{stable} with a 2.5~m line. HRMA does not
assume a plumbing layout on your behalf; if the length matters to you,
enter it.
\end{notebox}

\subsection{The finite-element panels}

Three FEA panels sit outside the Dock, anchored to the pages that can use
them:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em]
  \item \textbf{Axisymmetric wall structural FEA} (hybrid and liquid): von
  Mises stress field, safety-factor field, element-quality map and
  convergence history. If one of the four plots is missing, the reason is
  reported: a missing material record blocks the safety-factor field, and a
  solve that converged in a single round has no history to plot.
  \item \textbf{Transient wall thermal FEA} (hybrid): the wall temperature
  field, the inner-wall history and the time history. Its ambient
  temperature field arrives pre-filled. When the bridge cannot model your
  case, the refusal is now the answer: the request is rejected and the
  missing input is named, instead of a refusal arriving inside a
  ``success'' envelope. A reader that treated a successful HTTP status as
  ``the analysis ran'' has to read the verdict itself.
  \item \textbf{Plane-strain grain FEA} (solid): the acceptance criterion is
  the port bore strain, not the peak von Mises stress, and the badges say so.
\end{itemize}

\begin{notebox}
\small\color{hrmadark}
\textbf{Two ways a finite-element verdict is withheld.} If the closure base
placement margin exceeds the mesher's own thickness tolerance, the safety
factor is still published but \emph{no verdict is issued}, and the reason is
listed. If the thin-wall premise breaks (the thickness-to-radius ratio
exceeds the thin-wall limit), the panel shows a thin-wall badge. When the
ratio could not be measured at all, no warning is printed rather than a
reassuring one.
\end{notebox}

\section{Solid and Liquid Motors}\label{sec:solidliquid}

The solid and liquid pages follow the pattern of Section~\ref{sec:example}. What changes is
the input set and a handful of verdicts specific to each motor type.

\subsection{Solid motors}

The solid page is organized in nine numbered sections: \emph{0. Propellant
Catalogue}, \emph{1. Propellant Properties}, \emph{2. Grain Geometry},
\emph{3. Combustion Chamber and Casing}, \emph{4. Nozzle}, \emph{5.
Efficiency and Empirical Coefficients}, \emph{6. Initial \& Environmental
Conditions}, \emph{7. Mass \& Weight Breakdown}, and \emph{8. Measurement /
Test Equipment}.

\textbf{The propellant catalogue} is the fastest way to start. Filter by
family (composite, sugar KN-based, double base, other), pick a row, and its
validated properties load into the form. Three rules govern it, and they are
printed on the page:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em]
  \item Every loaded value stays editable; the catalogue only fills the
  fields.
  \item A field you type into is marked as overridden and is never
  overwritten by a later catalogue load. The page counts your overrides and
  offers either \emph{Reset to catalogue values} or \emph{Keep my edits}.
  \item The burn rate law is stated in the units the solver uses:
  $r\,[\mathrm{m/s}] = a \times (P\,[\mathrm{bar}])^{n}$.
\end{itemize}

\begin{notebox}
\small\color{hrmadark}
\textbf{An untouched field no longer overrides the catalogue.} The form used
to send a burn-rate coefficient even when you had not typed one, which
defeated the catalogue resolution on the server and produced a different
thrust curve together with a spurious off-catalogue warning. Untouched
fields are not sent; the server resolves the catalogue value and declares
where it came from.
\end{notebox}

\textbf{Grain geometry} offers BATES (cylindrical), star, finocyl, slotted,
wagon wheel and end burner, with the number of grains, outer diameter, core
diameter and grain length. Web thickness is a derived output, not an input:
if you enter a web thickness that disagrees with the value implied by the
geometry, the solver uses the geometric value and the warning quotes both.
The same rule applies to the case free volume.

\textbf{The two-phase correction status} is the one verdict to read before
quoting a solid Isp. Under the Isp cards the page prints a status band, and
it is honest about three different situations:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em]
  \item The two-phase loss factor field is \emph{empty by default}. Left
  empty, the solver applies no user two-phase factor and declares that it
  did not.
  \item The published Isp therefore carries \textbf{no verified two-phase
  (particle lag) correction}. The status appears in the API response, in the
  band on the page, and in the PDF report, and the warning tells you plainly
  not to read that Isp as a verified delivered performance.
  \item If the solver publishes no status at all, the band says \emph{not
  published by the solver} rather than assuming that the correction was
  applied or was unnecessary.
\end{itemize}

\begin{warnbox}
\small\color{hrmadark}
\textbf{A diagnostic that is now withheld outside its anchor window.} The
module's own linear two-phase loss estimate is a diagnostic: it is reported,
never applied. For propellants whose particle mass fraction falls outside
the window the anchoring reference figure covers, the number is now withheld
with the reason named, instead of being extrapolated. KN-sugar propellants
and black powder fall outside that window; APCP and double base fall inside
it and are unchanged.
\end{warnbox}

Two further changes affect the numbers you will read. The thermochemistry of
the KNDX and KNSB records now comes from the same two-phase equilibrium
basis as the other sugar propellants, so the four sugar records share one
verdict class; the measured effect is a small rise in $c^*$ and in delivered
Isp. And a propellant with no record in HRMA is not silently approximated:
the warning names the propellant, lists the known ones, and states that the
grain mechanics and two-phase results are not specific to your propellant.


\subsubsection{Choosing the grain geometry}

The geometry decides the shape of the thrust curve, and the program will not
choose it for you. Six entries, and the choice is made on burning-area history
rather than on manufacturing convenience:

\begin{center}
\footnotesize
\begin{tabular}{@{}>{\raggedright}p{3.0cm} >{\raggedright}p{4.8cm} p{6.0cm}@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Grain Type} & \textbf{Burning-area history} & \textbf{Where it fits} \\
\midrule
BATES (Cylindrical) & Burns outward from the core and inward from the two ends.
With several segments the ends compensate the growing core & The default
starting point. Simple to cast and to model \\
Star & The star points burn away first, so the area can be held nearly constant
& When you want flat pressure without segmenting \\
Finocyl & A cylindrical bore with fins at one end & High initial area with a

\end{center}

The geometry fields are \emph{Number of Grains N}, \emph{Outer Diameter (mm)},
\emph{Core Diameter (mm)} and \emph{Grain Length (mm)}, plus the shape-specific
fields: star point count, point radius and fillet radius; fin count, width and
length; slot count, width and depth. Face inhibition is selected separately for
the front face, the rear face and the outer surface, and it changes the burning
area directly, so it changes the pressure curve as much as the shape does.

\begin{notebox}
\small\color{hrmadark}
\textbf{Web thickness and case free volume are outputs.} Both fields exist, and
both are derived from the geometry. If what you type disagrees with what the
geometry implies, the solver uses the geometric value and the warning quotes
both numbers. Use the fields to check your understanding of the grain, not to
override it.
\end{notebox}

\subsubsection{A worked solid design}

This is the page's own starting configuration, stated as a design so you can see
which number drives which. Load a catalogue row first; the values below are what
the form arrives with.

\begin{center}
\footnotesize
\begin{tabular}{@{}>{\raggedright}p{5.4cm} >{\raggedright}p{2.4cm} p{6.0cm}@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Field} & \textbf{Value} & \textbf{Why it is the driver} \\
\midrule
Propellant Name/Formulation & APCP & Sets density, flame temperature, molecular

\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\textbf{What to expect, and in which order to read it.} The burn rate follows
$r\,[\mathrm{m/s}] = a \times (P\,[\mathrm{bar}])^{n}$, so chamber pressure and
burning area are coupled through the throat: a larger burning area raises the
pressure, which raises the burn rate, which raises the area again on a
progressive grain. Read in this order.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.6em]
  \item \textbf{The pressure curve.} If it climbs steeply, the grain is
  progressive; either segment it, inhibit a face, or change the shape.
  \item \textbf{The two-phase status band under the specific-impulse cards.}
  Until you read it you do not know whether the specific impulse is a delivered
  number or a theoretical one.
  \item \textbf{The temperature coefficient.} The initial temperature is a real
  design input on a solid motor, not an environmental note: $\sigma$ is accepted
  up to 0.02 per kelvin, and a cold-soaked motor and a sun-soaked one are
  different motors.
  \item \textbf{The case margin.} Case thickness is accepted from 2 to 100~mm,
  the yield strength from 10 to 3000~MPa, and the safety factor from 1.1 to 10.
  The structural verdict uses your thickness, not your target.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{notebox}
\small\color{hrmadark}
\textbf{Small solid motors are over-predicted.} Below roughly 75~mm the
delivered specific impulse can be substantially optimistic, because two-phase
flow, heat loss and short-$L^{*}$ scale effects are not modeled. The 100~mm
grain above sits just above that line; a 54~mm research motor does not.
\end{notebox}

\subsection{Liquid engines}

The liquid page is organized in nine numbered sections: \emph{1. Propellant
Data (Fuel + Oxidizer)}, \emph{2. O/F Optimization Data}, \emph{3. Feed
System / Engine Cycle}, \emph{4. Injector System Parameters}, \emph{5.
Combustion Chamber Design}, \emph{6. Nozzle \& Cooling System}, \emph{7.
Startup \& Transient Analysis}, \emph{8. Mass \& Structural Parameters}, and
\emph{9. Environment \& Test Conditions}.

\textbf{The engine cycle} is the choice that reshapes the rest of the page:
\emph{Pressure-Fed}, \emph{Gas Generator}, \emph{Staged Combustion},
\emph{Full-Flow Staged Combustion}, \emph{Expander Cycle} and \emph{Tap-Off
Cycle}. A power-balance solver checks whether the
pump and turbine powers close, and the thermochemistry is solved at the
engine's real chamber pressure, mixture ratio and expansion ratio.

Read these verdicts carefully on this page:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em]
  \item \textbf{The vacuum Isp band.} The interface checks how far the
  vacuum Isp sits above the sea-level value and compares it against the band
  expected from the ambient-pressure term of the thrust coefficient. Outside
  that band the warning prints both Isp values, the percentage, the accepted
  band and the relation it comes from, and tells you to check the expansion
  ratio and the ambient pressure you entered before using those two numbers.
  \item \textbf{A capped expansion ratio is not a matched nozzle.} If the
  solved optimum expansion ratio is clamped at the edge of the accepted
  band, the warning states which edge, what the clamped value is, and that
  the nozzle is no longer matched to the design altitude.
  \item \textbf{Feed pressure against the chamber.} A pressure-fed cycle
  whose tank pressure is below what the lines and injector need is named as
  such, with both the entered and the required pressure. The same check runs
  against the required pump discharge pressure in a pumped cycle.
  \item \textbf{Where the tank pressure is read.} In a turbopump-fed cycle
  the injector is fed by the pump, not by the tank: the tank is pressurized
  only for pump suction margin, and the solver sizes it itself and publishes
  it. A tank pressure sent into that cycle would change no published number,
  so the request is refused and the field you should be using is named.
  \item \textbf{Pump suction margin.} If the required pump NPSH exceeds what
  is available at the assumed tank pressure and suction-line losses, the
  warning gives both values and the two ways out (raise tank pressure or add
  an inducer). Where no vapor-pressure record exists for the propellant, the
  chain says which saturation assumption it used instead of a measured
  value.
  \item \textbf{Dump cooling publishes nothing it does not model.} Selecting
  dump cooling used to hand you the regenerative jacket's flow and pressure
  drop under a regenerative label. There is no bleed-fraction model for dump
  cooling, so the flow and pressure drop are now empty, the channel count is
  zero, and the provenance states that the bleed is not modelled.
  \item \textbf{A non-converged power balance has no residual.} The residual
  is the solver's own convergence remainder, not an independent cross-check,
  and when the cycle does not converge the field is empty with the reason
  given.
  \item \textbf{Staged-combustion pump head.} The required pump discharge
  pressure is sized on the main stage. If you compare against an older
  release for the same design, expect a lower discharge pressure; the power
  split was always right, the head was not.
\end{itemize}

\begin{notebox}
\small\color{hrmadark}
\textbf{Entered values that are comparison only.} Some liquid fields are
computed by the solver from the injector pressure drop, discharge
coefficient and mass flow. If you enter one of them, the warning tells you
which value the solver computed and states that your entry is reported for
comparison only. It is not silently ignored, and it does not silently win.
\end{notebox}

\textbf{Reading the cooling verdict.} The cooling type changes what the solver
will publish, and one option publishes deliberately less than the others.
Regenerative cooling gives you channel flow, velocity, pressure drop and a wall

for it, so the flow and pressure drop are empty, the channel count is zero, and
the provenance states that the bleed is not modeled. That is not a gap in the
page, it is the model declining to publish a regenerative jacket's numbers under
a dump-cooling label.

\textbf{Reading the pump verdicts.} Three of them travel together:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em]
  \item \textbf{Required pump discharge pressure} against your feed pressure.
  In a staged-combustion cycle it is sized on the main stage; if you compare
  against an older release for the same design, expect a lower discharge
  pressure than before.
  \item \textbf{NPSH available against NPSH required.} If the requirement
  exceeds what the tank pressure and the suction-line losses provide, the warning
  gives both values and the two ways out: raise the tank pressure, or add an
  inducer. Where no vapor-pressure record exists for the propellant, the chain
  states which saturation assumption it used instead of a measured value.
  \item \textbf{The vacuum against sea-level specific impulse gap.} The page
  checks the gap against the band implied by the ambient-pressure term of the
  thrust coefficient and, outside it, prints both values, the percentage, the
  band and the relation, and tells you to check the expansion ratio and the
  ambient pressure before using either number.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{What the exports refuse to draw}

The same fail-closed rule governs the exports, and it is the reason an
export sometimes contains fewer parts than you expected:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em]
  \item \textbf{The injector plate is drawn only with an earned thickness.}
  HRMA does not derive the plate thickness on its own; it comes from the
  injector panel. If it did not, the plate is left out of both the STL and
  the STEP package rather than drawn at a substituted thickness. This is why
  a complete liquid-engine package can now be produced where it previously
  failed: the correct behavior is to omit the part, not to refuse the whole
  package or to invent the dimension.
  \item \textbf{Geometry that the mesh does not represent is named.} An
  export rejection lists the fields it is missing, with a reason and the
  value you submitted for each, and where several addresses would be
  acceptable it lists them. A machine-readable code travels with the
  rejection and the English sentence travels next to it.
  \item \textbf{Downloads are direct.} In this release every export file
  is downloaded straight to your computer; there is no server-side link that
  expires later. If a file does not download, the message names the reason
  (missing geometry, an unearned dimension, a rejected field) and asks you to
  generate the package again. The application never invents a lifetime or a
  deadline.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{What the reports contain}

The PDF report covers the design, the performance, and the safety
assessment. That last section is worth a note: it has carried a heading for
a long time, but nothing filled it in, so it read as not evaluated and all
six hazard blocks were dropped. The server now runs the safety analysis
itself, once, from exactly the motor data the report renders, so the
document and the verdict cannot come from two different runs. Explosive,
toxic and fire hazards, required protective equipment, emergency response
and operational safety all reach the document, and a provenance line states
which field each input was resolved from.

The same rule closes the other way: if the report carries no usable chamber
pressure, propellant mass, chamber diameter or wall thickness, \emph{no
verdict is produced} and the missing measure is named. A safety verdict is
not computed from assumed dimensions.

\begin{notebox}
\small\color{hrmadark}
\textbf{The \texttt{.eng} file carries the two-phase status.} The delivered
Isp in the motor file is accompanied by the two-phase correction status in
the file's comment header, for solid, hybrid and liquid motors alike. If you
hand the file to a flight simulator, the qualification of the Isp travels
with it.
\end{notebox}

\begin{notebox}
\small\color{hrmadark}
\textbf{If the PDF fails its own check.} The report is verified after it is
built. If that check fails, HRMA downloads a plain-text report instead and
tells you it did, rather than handing you a PDF it could not verify.
\end{notebox}

\section{Input Rules and Error Messages}\label{sec:inputrules}

This section explains what the program refuses, why, and what the refusal
looks like on screen. Most support questions come down to one of these
rules.

\subsection{An empty field is not a zero}

Empty fields are not sent to the server. The point is that the server can
then distinguish ``the user left this out'' from ``the user set this
deliberately'', and answer accordingly: apply a canonical default and name
it, or refuse and name the field it needs. Three concrete consequences:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em]
  \item The eight vehicle and environment fields on the hybrid page (vehicle
  mass, reference area, drag coefficient, launch angle and altitude, wind
  speed and direction, oxidizer density) go unsent when empty rather than as
  an explicit null.
  \item \textbf{A parametric sweep with no interval is refused.} If the
  start or end of the sweep is missing, no points are computed and the
  missing field is named. A curve drawn over an interval the server guessed
  but labelled with your parameter name would describe nothing. The step
  count, by contrast, has a canonical default, and the endpoint declares it.
  \item The oxidizer density field is filled by the server. If it cannot be,
  the field stays empty with a hint naming the source, rather than carrying
  a static number that matches no storage state.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Values are checked against published bands}

Out-of-band values are rejected with the band named; they are not clamped
into range. The bands come from one table, so the form and the endpoint
cannot drift apart:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}>{\raggedright}p{5.0cm} >{\raggedright}p{4.0cm} p{5.0cm}@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Quantity} & \textbf{Accepted band} & \textbf{Where it applies} \\
\midrule
Chamber pressure (hybrid) & 1--500 bar & Hybrid preliminary geometry and the
optimum O/F search \\
Chamber pressure (solid) & 5--200 bar & Solid motor calculation and the
plane-strain grain solve \\
Chamber pressure (liquid) & 10--500 bar & Liquid engine calculation \\
Thrust (hybrid) & 1--10\textsuperscript{7} N & Hybrid preliminary geometry \\
Thrust (liquid) & 100--10\textsuperscript{7} N & Liquid engine calculation \\
Burn time & 0.1--1000 s & Hybrid preliminary geometry \\
O/F ratio & 0.1--50 & Hybrid preliminary geometry \\
Mixture ratio (liquid) & 0.5--20 & Liquid engine calculation \\
Chamber temperature & 300--6000 K & Hybrid geometry and the advanced
heat-transfer branch \\
Ambient temperature & 180--340 K & Quick geometry and the thermal FEA \\
Ratio of specific heats & 1.05--1.67 & Bartz heat-transfer chain \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

A field that genuinely has no defensible band says so rather than carrying
an invented one, and a rejection body states the band it applied together
with the basis of that band.

\subsection{A computed result is not an input}

Some names appear both as an input elsewhere and as an output here. Sending
the oxidizer mass flow into the main hybrid calculation is refused, because
that endpoint \emph{publishes} the oxidizer mass flow: it is a result, and
accepting it would let you label the solver's own number as your input. The
rejection says where the quantity is published and where to go if you wanted
to set it.

\subsection{Number-like text is not a number}

The program accepts JSON numbers. It does not accept a number wrapped in
quotes, and it does not accept values that are not finite. All the main
calculation endpoints use the same vocabulary for the same defect, so the
message you get does not depend on which page you were on:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}>{\raggedright}p{6.0cm} p{8.0cm}@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{What you sent} & \textbf{Reason reported} \\
\midrule
\texttt{NaN} or infinity & the value is not finite \\
A number in quotes, such as \texttt{"20.0"} & the value is not a JSON
number \\
\texttt{true} or \texttt{false} & a boolean is not a number \\
Text that is not a number at all & the field name with the reason \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The tank temperature is a good example of why this matters. A single request
carrying a temperature as text once gave one value to the motor and a
different one to the solver, a split of more than forty kelvin, with no
warning at all. The temperature is now resolved once, together with its
alias, and text is refused.

\subsection{What a rejection looks like on screen}

When the server refuses a request, the page shows the server's own words,
assembled in a fixed order:

\begin{center}
\texttt{[machine\_code] The sentence explaining the refusal · field
(reason), field (reason) · missing fields}
\end{center}

Nothing in that line is written by the page. If the body cannot be read at
all, you get the bare HTTP status and nothing more, because a plausible but
invented reason would be worse than none. When a panel refuses to run at
all, it names the empty fields and states that the request was not sent.

label is always right; your memory of the other page is not. After every change,
read \emph{What changed} before the headline number: a change that moved nothing
usually means the field you edited was not read by that endpoint.

\section{Scope and Limits (Please Read)}\label{sec:scope}

HRMA is a capable preliminary-design and educational tool. Knowing where it
stops is part of using it.

\subsection{What the models are}

The solvers are closed-form relations and one-dimensional correlations.
Turbopump maps, cavitation, rotordynamics, bearing life, startup and
shutdown transients, and two- or three-dimensional conjugate heat transfer
are not present. The nozzle CFD is an Euler solver with shock capturing; it
is not a validated viscous design tool, and it states that in its own badges.

\subsection{What has been measured}

HRMA carries a correlation runner that scores the model against a database
of real experiments and regenerates its own figures. The table below is the
main layer of that run, as published in
\texttt{docs/VALIDATION\_STATUS.md}. ``Bias'' is the signed mean of
(prediction minus measurement) divided by measurement; ``Median APE'' is the
median absolute percentage error; \emph{N} is the number of scored records.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}>{\raggedright}p{2.4cm} >{\raggedright}p{4.4cm} c c c@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Motor} & \textbf{Quantity} & \textbf{N} & \textbf{Bias \%} &
\textbf{Median APE \%} \\
\midrule
hybrid & characteristic velocity $c^*$ & 18 & $-0.1$ & 2.3 \\
hybrid & specific impulse & 18 & $+8.2$ & 7.7 \\
hybrid & chamber pressure & 35 & $+16.7$ & 13.5 \\
hybrid & final port diameter & 18 & $-9.4$ & 10.1 \\
hybrid & regression rate & 35 & $-24.9$ & 36.7 \\
liquid & sea-level specific impulse & 4 & $+2.0$ & 2.0 \\
liquid & vacuum specific impulse & 14 & $+0.8$ & 1.0 \\
liquid & vacuum thrust & 4 & $-0.4$ & 0.2 \\
solid & burn rate & 27 & $-0.4$ & 0.5 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Read that table with three cautions, all of which the source document states
itself:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em]
  \item \textbf{Some rows are not independent.} The solid burn-rate row is
  scored against the fit's own source data, which makes it an implementation
  check rather than an independent validation. The vacuum-thrust row carries
  a curator caveat on every record.
  \item \textbf{Good agreement at a known point is not prediction from
  scratch.} Reproducing vacuum Isp to about one percent \emph{given} the
  real operating point and published geometry does not mean HRMA predicts
  the flight performance of an unknown motor to that error.
  \item \textbf{Hybrid regression rate is the weak link, and the aggregate
  cell hides which half you are in.} The $-24.9$ percent figure is the mean
  of two populations that behave nothing alike. Scored against the
  Karabeyoglu paraffin/GOX campaign ($N = 17$) the model sits on its own
  published law: bias $+0.4$ percent, median APE 4.7 percent. Scored against
  the Rezaei low-flux HTPB/N\textsubscript{2}O campaign ($N = 18$) it
  under-predicts by roughly a factor of two: bias $-48.7$ percent, median
  APE 49.3 percent. That second half is a documented model limit rather than
  a data error --- one published $a$ and $n$ per fuel, a correlation whose
  validity band is about 10 to 30~g/cm\textsuperscript{2}$\cdot$s, and
  radiation plus small-motor effects that dominate below it. No coefficient
  was tuned to hide the gap, which is why the aggregate reads worse than a
  tuned model would.

  \textbf{The design rule that follows:} before you trust a regression rate,
  read your own oxidizer port mass flux off the results. If it is inside the
  correlation band, the number is trustworthy to a few percent. If you are
  below it --- which is where most student and amateur
  N\textsubscript{2}O/HTPB motors actually sit --- assume the program is
  under-predicting regression. Follow that through the chain: fuel flow
  $\dot m_f = \rho_f A_b \dot r$ is under-predicted with it, so the reported
  O/F is too high and the real motor runs richer than the report says; the
  port opens faster than predicted, so the real final port diameter is
  larger; and the web can burn through before the burn time you entered.
  The independently measured final-port-diameter row in the same table
  ($-9.4$ percent) is the same error seen from the geometry side, which is
  a useful consistency check on this reading. Fit your own $a$
  and $n$ from static fires and enter them in \emph{Fuel Configuration};
  that is the one input that moves this error, and it is why the field
  exists.
\end{itemize}

Outliers are flagged, never dropped, and records the model's own declared
scope does not cover are reported in a separate layer: measured and
withheld, not unmeasured.

\subsection{Known limitations}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em]
  \item \textbf{Liquid delivered Isp is idealized.} The liquid figures are
  optimum-expansion values; real engines deliver noticeably less because of
  finite expansion ratio and nozzle losses. The thermochemistry is sound;
  the delivered-Isp figures are optimistic upper bounds.
  \item \textbf{Small solid motors are over-predicted.} Below roughly 75~mm,
  delivered Isp can be substantially optimistic, because two-phase flow,
  heat loss and short-L* scale effects are not modelled.
  \item \textbf{Heat transfer is a Bartz-level estimate.} The gas-side
  coefficient and wall temperature are the right order of magnitude and
  conservative, but a real burn-through margin needs thermocouple data.
  \item \textbf{Combustion instability, ignition transients and hard starts}
  are outside the program entirely. Delivered $c^*$ efficiency can only be
  established by static fire.
  \item \textbf{Two-dimensional CFD with back pressure.} In a nozzle CFD run
  with a back pressure (\texttt{POST /api/cfd/nozzle}, \texttt{Pb\_Pa}) the
  outlet boundary condition imposes the back pressure also on cells where the
  flow reverses. In shocked, over-expanded cases the final field can show
  backflow at the outlet. These cases already return ``not converged /
  plateau'' and a \texttt{suspect} separation verdict; none is presented as
  verified. In these runs treat the wall pressure near the outlet and the
  separation output as qualitative.
  \item \textbf{Supercritical hydrogen coolant velocity.} Channel sizing also
  applies the 40~m/s liquid coolant velocity target (NASA SP-8087) to
  supercritical hydrogen; a hydrogen-specific target is planned for 3.1. The
  result errs on the safe side: deeper channel, lower coolant-side heat
  transfer, hotter predicted wall. So it does not produce a false SAFE.
  \item \textbf{Hybrid TNT equivalent in the API and the PDF report.} The
  derived factor is per kilogram of oxidizer; but since the endpoint and the
  report carry no oxidizer mass input, the factor is applied to the total
  propellant mass. The result is high on the cautious side. The safety panel
  does not evaluate the TNT equivalent for hybrids.
  \item \textbf{Star and finocyl grains are not exported to CAD.} The CAD core
  cannot yet draw these sections faithfully; the export is refused with the
  reason.
  \item \textbf{The assembly STL is not a closed solid.} Use STEP for CAD
  work.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Not in this release}

The following are \emph{not} in v3.0; they are planned for 3.1 and later:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em]
  \item The C++ solver core; 3D CFD and 3D FEA; reacting (combustion) CFD;
  modal FEA.
  \item CAD in hand-drawn detail: injector head and dome, flanges and bolt
  circle, O-ring grooves, ports, regenerative cooling channels, the real port
  section, a closed STL.
  \item Model and CAD of system parts: turbopump, preburner, feed lines,
  valves, gimbal; exhaust plume visuals. The turbopump power balance in the
  cycle panel is a cycle calculation, not a pump design.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Verdicts this version deliberately does not publish}

Knowing which verdicts are withheld is as useful as knowing which are given.
In this version the following are stated rather than answered:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em]
  \item \textbf{CFD convergence outside the self-consistency gate:} no
  verdict published.
  \item \textbf{Buckling without a declared external-pressure load case:}
  not evaluated, with the uncovered modes named.
  \item \textbf{Two-phase correction on solid Isp:} not verified, on every
  surface that publishes the Isp.
  \item \textbf{The linear two-phase diagnostic outside its anchor window:}
  withheld with the reason.
  \item \textbf{Throat erosion for a material with no published coefficient
  band} (tungsten): no published data.
  \item \textbf{Dump cooling jacket flow and pressure drop:} not modelled.
  \item \textbf{Water hammer for hydrogen peroxide:} no citable vapor
  pressure, so no block is produced.
  \item \textbf{Ablation liner thickness when there is no net heating:} not
  published, because the governing limit is outside this level of model.
  \item \textbf{A finite-element verdict when the closure base margin
  exceeds the mesher's tolerance:} withheld while the number is still shown.
  \item \textbf{Regulatory compliance:} not evaluated, in any form.
\end{itemize}

\begin{warnbox}
\small\color{hrmadark}
\textbf{The golden rule.} HRMA is a starting point. Cross-check every
critical result with an independent code (CEA / RPA / openMotor) and, where
possible, with a static fire. Knowing how to drive the program is not, by
itself, being a rocket engine engineer.
\end{warnbox}

\section{Automatic Updates and Troubleshooting}\label{sec:updates}

\subsection{How updating works}

At startup, HRMA asks GitHub whether a newer version exists. If one does, a
window appears offering \textbf{Update now}, \textbf{Later}, or \textbf{Skip
this version}.

\emph{Update now} downloads the installer with a progress indicator. When
the download finishes, HRMA closes and the installation runs; the
application reopens by itself when it is done. On macOS this usually takes a
few minutes, and the message says so: do not open HRMA by hand while it is
working.

The flow is written to survive a bad network:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em]
  \item If the download is slow or interrupted, the window offers a browser
  link so you can fetch the installer yourself.
  \item If the installer cannot be started, the message tells you to open
  the downloaded file from your Downloads folder.
  \item If GitHub rate-limits your network, which happens on shared
  connections, HRMA says so and falls back to the ordinary releases page,
  which it can still read.
  \item The window states where the version information came from: the
  Releases API, or the releases page as a fallback.
\end{itemize}

\emph{Skip this version} suppresses that one version. Settings lists the
skipped version and \emph{Reset skipped version} makes it available again.
\emph{Check for Updates\ldots} in the window menu and \emph{Check now} in
Settings both run the check on demand.

\section{Appendix B: Glossary}\label{sec:appb}

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}>{\raggedright}p{4.4cm} p{9.6cm}@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Term} & \textbf{Meaning as used in HRMA} \\
\midrule
Thrust & The force the motor produces, in newtons. \\
Total impulse & Thrust integrated over the burn, in newton-seconds; the
size of the motor in one number. \\
Specific impulse (Isp) & Impulse per unit weight of propellant, in seconds,
referenced to the standard $g_0$ (not the local gravity the Launch Site page
reports). \\
Characteristic velocity ($c^*$) & The combustion side of performance,
independent of the nozzle. Errors here are thermochemistry errors. \\
Thrust coefficient ($C_F$) & The nozzle side of performance. Isp is the
product of the two divided by $g_0$. \\
Chamber pressure & The absolute pressure in the combustion chamber, in bar
throughout the interface. \\
O/F ratio & Oxidizer-to-fuel mass ratio. Called mixture ratio on the liquid
page. \\
Expansion ratio ($\varepsilon$, Ae/At) & Nozzle exit area divided by throat
area. \\
Contraction ratio (Ac/At) & Chamber cross-section divided by throat area. \\
Characteristic length (L*) & Chamber volume divided by throat area; sets how
long the gas stays in the chamber. \\
Nozzle & The convergent-divergent duct: throat, divergent section, exit.
Contour options are conical, bell and parabolic. \\
Divergence loss & The thrust lost because the exit flow is not purely
axial. \\
Regression rate & How fast a hybrid fuel surface recedes, usually written
$r = a\,G^{n}$ against the oxidizer mass flux $G$. \\
Burn rate & The solid-propellant equivalent, written
$r = a\,P^{n}$ against chamber pressure in bar. \\
Web thickness & The propellant thickness that has to burn through; on the
solid page it is derived from the grain geometry, not entered. \\
Port & The cavity the gas flows through inside the grain; its diameter grows
during the burn. \\
Grain & The cast solid propellant or hybrid fuel body. \\
Two-phase loss & The performance lost because condensed particles lag the
gas. In HRMA it is a diagnostic unless you supply a factor, and its status is
always published. \\
Bartz correlation & The standard relation for the gas-side heat transfer
coefficient; the basis of the thermal results. \\
NPSH & Net positive suction head, the margin that keeps a pump from
cavitating. \\
Chug & The low-frequency feed-coupled instability the combustion stability
panel evaluates. \\
Water hammer & The pressure surge produced when a valve closes on a flowing
line. \\
Fail closed & The program's habit of refusing, withholding or declaring
rather than substituting a value it cannot justify. \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\textbf{Two different refusals hide in that table.} \emph{Core Diameter} is a
hard gate: outside its band the request is refused outright and the answer
quotes the band it computed for your chamber. The four material rows above

it quietly becomes the catalogue density and every mass in the report
belongs to a different propellant than the one you typed.

\textbf{The chamber diameter band is declared, not enforced.} A value
outside 10--5000~mm does not refuse the request; it is ignored, the diameter
is derived from the contraction ratio instead, and the answer names the
field. A value \emph{inside} the band is not safe either: if it pushes the
contraction ratio outside its own band, it is dropped the same way and a
second warning says so. On a chamber sized around a 33~mm throat, for
example, 100 and 200~mm are applied as typed while 500 and 1000~mm are
dropped back to the derived 75.7~mm. Treat the number you type here as a
request, and read the published chamber diameter back before you trust it.

\textbf{Chamber pressure is not on this list, and that is not an omission.}
No post-calculation warning is raised on chamber pressure at all. Its real
gates sit on the form side and are refusals, not warnings: the solid
endpoint accepts 5--200~bar, the liquid endpoint 10--500~bar, and the hybrid
quick-geometry and find-optimum endpoints 1--500~bar, each refusing an
out-of-band value with HTTP~400 and naming the band. The hybrid calculation
form has no chamber-pressure band of its own; the ``at least 1'' floor you
meet on screen is the input widget's own minimum. If you press
\emph{Calculate} at 500~bar you will still see a warning, but read it
carefully: it comes from the thrust-coefficient identity
$C_F \equiv F/(A_t P_c)$, which is a consistency check between three numbers
you supplied, not a statement that 500~bar is out of range.
